# Content, Ordering, Layout, and Length

Covers research areas 1–4 from the brief: what to include/exclude by seniority,
section ordering and prioritization, layout/typography/length, and honest
emphasis techniques.

## Key findings

### 1. What to include vs. exclude shifts by seniority

- **Entry-level**: summaries add little because there's little to summarize;
  GPA and coursework carry more weight in the absence of work history; a
  projects/GitHub section does real work standing in for missing experience.
  (Harvard FAS, huyenchip.com career-advice post)
- **Mid-level IC (Pradeep's bracket)**: work experience should be "the bulk of
  your resume" — Google recruiting guidance places heavy weight on measurable
  accomplishments in past roles over a projects list. GPA becomes optional to
  irrelevant. Objective statements are considered outdated ("That was 1970. We
  are in 2022." — Erica Rivera, Google Senior Recruiter, quoted in Information
  Age/ACS). (ACS/Information Age; general Google-recruiter guidance)
- **Senior/staff**: a tight professional summary near the top starts pulling
  its weight because it compresses years of scope into a few lines before the
  reader commits to reading further; standalone "Projects" sections shrink or
  disappear in favor of a GitHub/portfolio link, since the work history itself
  should already demonstrate the scope.
- Across all levels: drop street address (city/state or city/country is
  enough), drop "References available on request," drop basic software
  literacy (Microsoft Office, email) as assumed baseline, and drop graduation
  dates for anyone more than a few years out of school to avoid inviting age
  bias. (ACS/Information Age — Google recruiter)

### 2. Ordering and prioritization for a technical/research IC

- Harvard's career-services guidance: list sections "in order of importance,"
  and within each section use reverse-chronological order (most recent
  first). For an IC with real shipped work, that means Experience outranks
  Education outranks everything else — flip that order only if the candidate
  is a current student or the target role explicitly weights the degree (e.g.
  academic/research-lab positions where the graduate program is the primary
  credential).
- Google recruiting guidance specifically tells engineering candidates to put
  hard skills / programming languages near the top of the resume, ahead of
  soft framing — recruiters and hiring managers are scanning for role-fit
  signals first.
- Chip Huyen (ML/infra hiring manager, huyenchip.com) makes an ordering point
  specific to technical IC roles: what she wants surfaced is not a bare list
  of technologies but the hardest problem solved and what was learned from it
  — meaning within a role's bullet list, the technically hardest, most
  differentiated accomplishment should lead, not be buried under routine
  duties.

### 3. Layout, typography, and length

- **Fonts**: standard, widely-supported fonts only — Arial, Calibri, Garamond,
  Times New Roman — at 10–12pt body text; name can go up to 14–16pt; avoid
  below 9pt or decorative fonts. Multiple ATS-mechanics sources converge on
  this range (see `ats-mechanics.md`), and it doubles as a human-readability
  recommendation (Tech Interview Handbook, Jobscan-adjacent sources).
- **Margins**: narrow margins (as low as 0.5") are an acceptable way to buy
  back space rather than shrinking the font further or using a header/footer
  (Tech Interview Handbook — the guide specifically recommends *not* using
  headers/footers to save space, and reducing margins instead, since
  header/footer content risks being dropped by parsers — see ATS file).
  Stanford's handout specifies 1" minimum margins as the more conservative
  default for a printed/reviewed resume.
- **Length**: this is the most evidence-backed area of the whole brief.
  - Rule of thumb across career centers: one page for early-career (<5 years);
    professionals with 5–20 years' experience commonly run 1.5–2 pages; no
    resume should exceed ~3 pages regardless of seniority.
  - TalentWorks (Resunate/TalentWorks job-search data team) analyzed 6,305
    real job applications across 66 industries and found resumes in the
    475–600 word range got roughly double the interview rate (8.2%) of
    resumes outside that band; going over ~600 words was associated with a
    marked drop in callback rate. Only ~23% of resumes in their sample fell in
    the optimal band, meaning most applicants are unknowingly self-sabotaging
    on length. This is an empirical study, not a style opinion, and it's the
    strongest single data point in this file.
  - At ~4 years of experience with four to five substantial roles/projects
    worth describing, Pradeep sits exactly at the boundary where a **one,
    tight page beats a loose two pages** — the data favors information
    density within the 475–600 word band over raw page count.
- **Whitespace**: eye-tracking research from TheLadders (hosted via Boston
  University; original TheLadders study on 6-second resume scans) found that
  resumes with cluttered layouts and insufficient white space performed worse
  in recruiter first-pass review than resumes with a clean F/E-pattern layout,
  clear section headers, and bolded titles. (Note: could not retrieve the full
  PDF text directly — it returned as corrupted binary — so this finding is
  reported at the level of the widely-cited summary rather than a page-by-page
  read of the primary document; treat as moderately, not fully, verified.)

### 4. Honest emphasis techniques (no hype language)

- Bold sparingly and specifically: numbers/results (percentages, deltas,
  scale) and job titles are the highest-value bolding targets, because
  they're literally what a time-pressured reader is scanning for. Keep any
  single bolded run to roughly five words or fewer so bolding stays a
  wayfinding device instead of becoming visual noise that cancels itself out.
- Bold a skill or technology only where it's doing real work in a bullet
  (i.e., contextually, inside an accomplishment sentence) rather than as a
  standalone tag — this reads as "this skill was actually used to produce
  this result," which is a stronger and more honest signal than a bolded
  skills-cloud.
- Harvard's guidance is explicit that "consistent" application of
  bold/italics/underlining matters as much as which words are chosen —
  inconsistent emphasis reads as sloppiness, not confidence.
- The honest version of "make strengths stand out" is structural, not verbal:
  put the most differentiated bullet first in each role, use white space and
  bullet-count discipline (3–5 bullets per role, capped at roughly two lines
  each per Stanford's handout) rather than superlative adjectives to create
  hierarchy.

## Distilled rules/heuristics

1. Experience section leads; Education follows; skills condensed into a
   scannable line or two, not a sprawling grid.
2. One tight page is achievable and preferable at ~4 years' experience if the
   writing stays in the empirically-supported 475–600 word band; do not
   default to two pages just because "senior" advice says it's allowed.
3. Standard font (10–12pt body), narrow-but-sane margins, no header/footer for
   load-bearing content, consistent bullet glyph throughout.
4. Within each role, lead with the single hardest/most differentiated
   accomplishment, not the most recent or most routine one.
5. Bold only numbers/results and job titles, applied consistently and
   sparingly — never bold adjectives or self-assessment language.
6. Cut: street address, "References available on request," graduation dates
   older than ~5 years, objective statements, basic-software-literacy lines.
7. 3–5 bullets per role, each ≤2 lines, each opening with a past-tense action
   verb, never with a date.
