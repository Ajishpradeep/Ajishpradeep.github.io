## 1. EU Blue Card — eligibility and thresholds by country

### 1.1 What the directive actually requires

Directive (EU) 2021/1883 (the "recast" Blue Card directive) replaced the 2009 directive.
Core rules, from the European Commission's own description:

- **Applies in 25 member states. Denmark and Ireland have opted out** — there is no Blue
  Card in Dublin or Copenhagen. Both operate purely national routes (§3).
- **Minimum contract duration reduced to 6 months** (was 12). Several member states still
  require 12 months in national law — Netherlands, Poland, France do; Germany, Belgium and
  Spain accept 6.
- **Salary threshold band: 1.0× to 1.6× the national average gross annual salary.** Member
  states pick a point in that band, which is why the spread below is so wide.
- **Higher professional qualification**: a post-secondary higher-education qualification of
  at least three years, *or* — new in the recast — equivalent **professional experience** in
  specified ICT occupations.
- **Intra-EU mobility after 12 months** in the first member state, without a repeat labour
  market test.

That last point matters strategically: **the Blue Card is portable in a way no national
permit is.** Twelve months in a lower-threshold country (Germany, Portugal, Poland, Spain)
buys simplified movement to a higher-threshold one (France, Belgium, Netherlands) later.
Entering Europe cheaply and moving up is a legitimate, directive-sanctioned strategy.

### 1.2 Does his Taiwanese MSc satisfy the higher-education requirement?

**Yes, in principle, everywhere.** Nothing in the directive or in any national
transposition restricts the qualification by country of issue. What varies is the
*evidence standard* — whether an authority accepts the diploma directly or demands a formal
comparability statement first. That is §2, and it is the only genuine friction point.

He also has a **fallback he probably does not know about**. Under German law
(§ 18g AufenthG, verified against the statutory text) a Blue Card can be granted **without
a degree at all** to applicants in ISCO-08 groups **133** (ICT service managers) or **25**
(ICT professionals) who have *"mindestens dreijährige Berufserfahrung in den letzten sieben
Jahren"* — at least three years of relevant experience in the last seven — with skills
comparable to a university qualification, paid at the 45.3% threshold. He satisfies that
independently of the MSc. **His degree recognition is therefore a convenience, not a single
point of failure, in Germany.** That is a meaningful risk reduction.

### 1.3 His occupation is a statutory shortage occupation in Germany

§ 18g AufenthG grants the reduced salary threshold to ISCO-08 groups **132, 133, 134, 21,
221, 222, 225, 226, 23 and 25**. An AI/computer-vision research engineer falls in **ISCO 25**
(ICT professionals — 2512/2519 software developers and analysts) or **ISCO 21** (science and
engineering professionals). **Both are on the list.** He gets the lower threshold on
occupation grounds alone, permanently — he does not need to rely on the "graduated within
3 years" concession, which for a 2023 graduate expires during 2026.

## 2. Degree recognition — the mechanics, and exactly what to do

### 2.1 What a ZAB Statement of Comparability is

The **Zentralstelle für ausländisches Bildungswesen (ZAB)**, the German Standing
Conference's Central Office for Foreign Education, issues a **Statement of Comparability
for Foreign Higher Education Qualifications** (*Zeugnisbewertung*). It is an official
federal certificate stating which German qualification a foreign degree compares to, and
confirming that the awarding institution is state-recognised in its home country.

| Item | Detail | Source date |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | **€208** (replacement certificate €104) | ZAB, Aug 2026 |
| Standard processing | **3 months** from complete documents + payment received | ZAB |
| **Fast-track** | **~2 weeks** where required for an **EU Blue Card** or the §81a Fast-Track Procedure for Skilled Workers | ZAB FAQ |
| Application | Online only. **BundID account required**, then Pre-Check → online form → PDF uploads → immediate online payment | ZAB |
| Documents | Must be **in the original language**. Translations may be required *in addition*, never instead. Scanned PDFs | ZAB |
| Validity | Indefinite. It does not expire | — |

### 2.2 Is it strictly required for a Blue Card?

**It depends on anabin, and the honest answer is: probably yes for him, and he should
assume yes.**

The German process is two-stage:

1. **anabin** (`anabin.kmk.org`) is the KMK's database of foreign institutions and degrees
   and is the binding reference for German authorities. If **both** the institution is
   listed with status **H+** (equivalent to a German *Hochschule*) **and** the specific
   degree is listed as *"entspricht"* / equivalent, the Ausländerbehörde or consulate can
   accept a printout of the anabin entries and **no ZAB statement is needed**.
2. If either entry is missing, ambiguous, or the institution shows **H+/-**, a **ZAB
   Statement of Comparability is required**.

The official guidance from `anerkennung-in-deutschland.de` states it plainly: a Statement
of Comparability is **required** "when entering Germany from a third country to work as a
skilled worker," and application to ZAB "may or must be made if no entry can be found in
the [anabin] database."

**On NTUT specifically: I could not confirm its anabin status.** anabin's institution
search is a session-driven form that does not survive automated retrieval, and its Taiwan
country page returned 404. This is a **gap he must close himself, and it takes ten minutes**
— see §7 Day 1. Priors: NTUT (國立臺北科技大學) is a national public university under
Taiwan's Ministry of Education, which is the fact pattern that produces **H+**. Taiwan's
higher education system is well-represented in anabin. But *"probably H+"* is not a basis
for a visa application, and even with H+ the **degree-level entry** is a separate lookup
that frequently comes back inconclusive for Taiwanese Master's programmes.

**Decision rule: apply for the ZAB statement regardless.** €208 and a form, against a
route-blocking risk. The statement is *also* the accepted proof for the **Opportunity Card**
and the **Skilled Worker** routes, and German employers and Ausländerbehörden find it far
easier to process than an anabin printout. There is no scenario where holding it hurts.

becomes the live target.

### 3.2 Germany — Skilled Immigration Act, and the §81a fast-track he should ask employers for

The Skilled Immigration Act (*Fachkräfteeinwanderungsgesetz*) reforms, fully in force since
mid-2024, give him three parallel German options:

- **EU Blue Card (§18g)** — primary. Covered above.
- **Skilled Worker with academic training (§18b)** — no salary threshold at all, but requires
  a **recognised** degree (i.e. the ZAB statement or a clean anabin entry) and the
  Bundesagentur für Arbeit's approval of employment terms. Useful if an offer lands below
  €45,934.20, which for a senior AI role it should not.
- **Opportunity Card (§20a)** — job-seeker route, §4.

**The under-used lever: the Accelerated Skilled Worker Procedure (§81a AufenthG).** The
**employer** enters an agreement with the local Ausländerbehörde, pays **€411**, and the
process runs on **statutory deadlines** — commonly **4–8 weeks** end to end (roughly 1–2
weeks document prep, 2–4 weeks Ausländerbehörde plus Bundesagentur für Arbeit pre-approval,
then **3 working days** for the consulate to issue after the appointment), versus 3–6 months
on the ordinary path. It also **unlocks the ZAB 2-week fast-track**.

This is a concrete, high-value thing to raise with a German employer: *"There is a €411
statutory fast-track that gets me on-site in about six weeks instead of four months."* It
reframes him from a delay risk into a candidate with a plan, and it costs the employer less
than one recruiter coffee. **Most German HR teams outside the large corporates do not know
§81a exists.** Naming it is a differentiator.

### 3.3 Ireland — Critical Skills Employment Permit

**Ireland is a genuinely strong option and the existing research under-weights it.**

- **Not a Blue Card country** (opt-out). The CSEP is the route.
- **His occupation is explicitly listed.** The Critical Skills Occupations List effective
  **13 May 2026 (SI 213 of 2026)** includes SOC **2136** *Programmers and software
  development professionals*, **2135** *IT business analysts, architects and systems
  designers*, **2139** *All other ICT professionals n.e.c.*, and **2133** *IT specialist
  managers* — all with **no additional conditions attached**. An AI/computer-vision research
  engineer sits squarely in 2136 or 2139.
- Note a trap in the adjacent engineering codes: SOC **2124** (electronics engineers) and
  **2126** (design and development engineers) carry **specialisation conditions**
  (chip design, test engineering, process automation, validation, power generation). If an
  employer classifies him as an engineer rather than an ICT professional, the conditions
  bite. **He should steer classification to 2136/2139.** This is a real, avoidable failure
  mode.
- **Salary: €40,904** for listed occupations with a relevant degree (€36,848 if qualified
  within the previous 12 months — not applicable to him). The €68,911 tier is for
  *unlisted* occupations only and is irrelevant here.
- **No Labour Market Needs Test.**
- **Contract: minimum 2 years.** He must stay with the initial employer **9 months**.
- **Fee €1,000** (90% refunded if refused), paid by the employer.
- **Immediate family reunification** — better than most of the EU.
- Applications should be filed **12 weeks** before the intended start date.
- **No employer licensing regime.** Any Irish employer registered with Revenue and the CRO
  can apply. The old **Trusted Partner Initiative** has effectively been folded into the
  Employment Permits Online System (EPOS) — DETE notes all employers now submit Revenue and
  CRO documentation to maintain a portal account, so there is **no separate Trusted Partner
  gate** to check. **Do not use "is it a Trusted Partner?" as a filter; it is obsolete.**

Ireland's drawback is real but narrow: the **two-year contract** is a bigger ask than a
German six-month one, and Irish permit processing has historically had visible backlogs.
Offsetting that, Dublin has the densest concentration of US tech EMEA engineering in Europe
and those employers file CSEPs routinely.

### 3.4 Netherlands — Highly Skilled Migrant (*kennismigrant*)

- **Salary (2026): €5,942/month** gross for age 30+, **€4,357/month** under 30 — both
  **excluding** the 8% holiday allowance. So the 30+ figure is ~€77k/yr all-in. Reduced
  criterion **€3,122/month** for recent graduates / orientation-year holders.
- **Blue Card in NL: €5,942/month**, reduced criterion **€4,754/month**. ⚠ **Time-sensitive
  and needs verification:** the reduced Blue Card criterion appears to be tied to graduating
  within the last three years. He graduated in **2023**; that window closes during **2026**.
  If the Dutch reduced rate matters to him, this is urgent. **Verify directly with IND
  before relying on it.**
- **No degree requirement** for HSM — the test is salary + market conformity. Salary is the
  proxy for skill. This makes HSM **easier than the Dutch Blue Card** for him: no Nuffic
  evaluation, no diploma scrutiny.
- **Decision period: 90 days statutory**, in practice often 2–4 weeks for recognised
  sponsors. **Fee €423**, paid by the employer.
- **The recognised-sponsor requirement is absolute.** Only IND-recognised sponsors can file.
  See §6.2.

### 3.5 Denmark

Denmark is outside the Blue Card and runs three relevant national schemes:

- **Fast-track scheme** — requires a **SIRI-certified employer**. 2026 pay-limit track
  **DKK 552,000/yr** (~€74k); supplementary pay-limit track **DKK 446,000/yr** (~€60k).
  Processing **1 month** (2 months if info requested); supplementary track 2 months.
  Fee **DKK 6,810**.
- **Positive List for People with a Higher Education** — updated **1 July 2026**, now
  **190 job titles**, including *Machine Learning Engineer*, *software developer*,
  *IT architect*, *data scientist*, *DevOps engineer*, *Cloud Architect*. No fixed salary
  floor, but pay must match "customary Danish standards." **This is the realistic Danish
  route for him** — the Positive List bypasses the high Pay Limit figure.
- **Pay Limit Scheme** — DKK 552,000, no occupation restriction.

Denmark's advantage: a **public register of certified companies** (§6.3). Its disadvantage:
the market is small and, per `european-market.md`, more conservative in hiring convention
than its reputation suggests.

### 3.6 Sweden — use the ordinary work permit, not the Blue Card

**A correction worth flagging.** Sweden's Blue Card threshold (**SEK 53,625/month** from
15 July 2026, = 1.25× average salary) is *far higher* than its **ordinary work permit**
threshold (**SEK 33,390/month** from 1 June 2026, = 90% of the SEK 37,100 median). Anyone
optimising for "Blue Card everywhere" would get this exactly backwards.

The ordinary Swedish permit does carry friction the Blue Card avoids: the position must be
advertised in Sweden and the EU/EEA for 10 days, and the relevant trade union is given the
opportunity to comment on the terms. Neither is a veto, but both add weeks and require an
employer who has done it before.

Sweden's Blue Card education test is notably generous: **180 higher-education credits
(3 years) OR at least 5 years of relevant professional experience.** He clears both.

### 3.7 Portugal and Spain

- **Portugal**: Blue Card threshold ~**€21,030** (2026) — the lowest in Western Europe.
  60-day processing, no labour market test. Also runs a **Skilled Job Seeker Visa**
  (Article 57-A) — see §4.3.
- **Spain**: **20-day maximum processing** — the fastest in the EU by a wide margin.
  Threshold €41,356.36 from 28 May 2026, with a **€31,415.94** reduced rate for shortage
  occupations in NCO groups 1–2. Three-year initial validity.

Both are administratively easy and commercially thin for 3D computer vision. Treat them as
**opportunistic**, not as a plan.

### 3.8 Poland

Threshold **PLN 13,355.34/month** (2026, = 150% of average national salary; the EU portal's
PLN 9,519.23 is stale). **Poland is the one Blue Card country in scope that applies a
labour market test**, which materially reduces employer appetite. 60-day processing,
12-month minimum contract. Deprioritise.

## 4. Job-seeker routes — entering to search rather than needing an offer

### 4.1 Germany — Opportunity Card (*Chancenkarte*, §20a AufenthG)

Two ways in:

- **Route A — recognised skilled worker.** Hold a degree that is **fully recognised in
  Germany** (ZAB statement or clean anabin entry) plus a vocational/academic qualification
  meeting the skilled-worker standard. **No points needed.**
- **Route B — points.** Score at least **6 points**, plus baseline language (**German A1
  or English B2**) and proof of funds.

Indicative points allocation (from secondary sources — the official Make it in Germany
portal was bot-blocked, so **verify before relying on the exact weights**): qualification
up to 4; recent professional experience 2–3; language German/English 1–3; age **2 points if
under 35, 1 point if 35–40**; previous stay in Germany of 6+ months in the last 5 years, 1.

**His likely score comfortably clears 6** on qualification + experience + English B2 alone,
before age is counted. But **Route A is the better door** for him — with the ZAB statement
he skips the points arithmetic entirely. That is a second reason the ZAB application is the
highest-leverage single item.

Terms: **valid up to 12 months**, **part-time work up to 20 hours/week** permitted, plus
**unlimited two-week trial employment** periods with prospective employers. Proof of funds
for 2026: approximately **€1,091/month (€13,092/yr)**, typically via a blocked account.

**Honest assessment of the odds.** The Opportunity Card is the strongest job-seeker
instrument in Europe and being *in Germany* materially improves interview conversion — it
removes the "when could you actually start?" objection that kills remote applicants. But it
is a **12-month, non-renewable clock funded out of his own savings**, with only 20 hours of
paid work permitted. For someone currently employed on an APRC in Taipei with a good
salary, **jumping to the Opportunity Card before exhausting the offer-first route is a
material downgrade in position.** The two-week trial-employment provision is the genuinely
clever part: it lets a hesitant German employer try him with essentially zero
administrative commitment, then convert to a Blue Card in-country without leaving Germany.

**Recommended use: as a fallback in month 9–12 if the offer-first campaign has stalled — not
as the opening move.**

### 4.2 UK — no job-seeker visa that fits

The UK's High Potential Individual (HPI) visa requires a degree from a university on the
Global Universities List (top ~50 worldwide by aggregated ranking) awarded in the last
5 years. **NTUT is not on that list.** HPI is closed to him. Global Talent is the UK answer
and it is better anyway — it carries work rights, not just search rights.

### 4.3 Portugal — Skilled Job Seeker Visa (Article 57-A)

**120 days**, extendable once by **60 days** via the AIMA portal. Requires a recognised
higher-education degree or equivalent professional qualification, an eligible skilled
profession (technology and engineering are in scope), and proof of funds of roughly
**three times the national minimum wage** (~€2,760 on 2026 figures). On securing a
contract, AIMA converts it to a **D3 residence permit** (1–2 years) without leaving the
country.

Cheap and low-risk relative to the German option, but 120 days is short and the Portuguese
market for his specialism is narrow. **Situational.**

### 4.4 Others

Sweden, the Netherlands, Ireland, Denmark, France and Belgium have **no general job-seeker
visa** for someone in his position. The Dutch **orientation year** (*zoekjaar*) is limited
to graduates of Dutch institutions and top-200 universities within 3 years of graduation —
**closed to him on both counts**.

## 5. The UK, in depth

### 5.1 Why Global Talent is the standout

It is the only route in this entire document that **requires no employer, no job offer, no
sponsor, and no salary threshold.** He can begin it this week, unilaterally, while employed
in Taipei. If endorsed, he can put on every application:

> Right to work in the UK — Global Talent visa. No sponsorship required.

That single line converts him from an administrative problem into a normal candidate across
the entire London/Cambridge/Bristol market — including PhysicsX, Graphcore and the London
targets already identified in `market-demand-2026.md`. **Nothing else in this research has
that leverage.**

### 5.2 Who endorses, and current process (as at Aug 2026)

**Tech Nation** is the endorsing body for **Digital Technology**. This survived Tech Nation's
2023 wind-down and subsequent revival. Since a **process simplification in August 2025**,
the application is filed on the **standard Home Office Stage 1 endorsement form on GOV.UK**,
and the Home Office forwards it to Tech Nation — not submitted to Tech Nation directly.

Four stages:

1. **Stage 1 — endorsement application** (self-filed, GOV.UK), fee **£561**
2. **Tech Nation review**, GOV.UK states **5–8 weeks** (Tech Nation says up to 8)
3. **Stage 2 — visa application**, within 3 months of endorsement, fee **£205**
4. **Home Office decision** — **3 weeks** from outside the UK, 8 weeks from inside

**Total £766** plus the **Immigration Health Surcharge at £1,035/year**. Choosing 5 years
up front means roughly **£766 + £5,175 ≈ £5,941**. That is the real cost and it is not
small. IHS can be paid for a shorter grant and topped up on extension.

### 5.3 Talent vs Promise — which should he apply under?

| | **Exceptional Talent** | **Exceptional Promise** |
|---|---|---|
| Standard | Recognised as a **leader** in digital technology in the last 5 years | Recognised as a **potential leader** in the last 5 years |
| Experience | Typically 5+ years in digital tech | Typically **5 years or fewer** |
| ILR | **3 years** | **5 years** |

**Recommendation: Exceptional Promise.** His ~3 years in AI puts him in Promise's window
even though total engineering experience is ~8 years, and "recognised as a leader" is an
evidentiary bar he cannot presently meet without first-author publications or a founder
role. **Applying for Talent and being refused is worse than applying for Promise and being
endorsed** — the 3-vs-5-year ILR difference is not worth the risk. Tech Nation's own framing
for Promise — early-career applicants with "strong academics or significant contributions to
innovative projects, plus endorsements from 3 senior stakeholders" — is close to a
description of his profile.

### 5.4 The criteria and how his evidence maps

He must satisfy **one mandatory criterion** (recognised as a leader / potential leader) **and
at least two of four optional criteria**:

1. Founder or senior executive of a **product-led digital technology company**
2. Work in a **novel** digital technology field or concept, or exceptional ability to
   advance the sector **outside his day job** (mentoring, open source, speaking)
3. **Significant technical, commercial or entrepreneurial contributions** at a digital
   technology company
4. **Academic contributions** — research published or endorsed by an expert

**His mapping:**

| Asset | Criterion | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **International innovation award — 1 of 3 winners from 638 proposals across 55 countries** | **Mandatory** (external recognition) | **Very strong.** This is precisely what "recognised by others" means. The selection ratio is objectively verifiable and quantified — exactly the "measurable impact" Tech Nation says is missing from most refused applications |
| **NVIDIA GTC 2025 technical poster** | **Optional 2** (advancing the sector outside employment) and partially **Optional 4** | **Strong.** GTC is the flagship industry conference in accelerated computing; a technical poster there is industry recognition, not academic publication — and Tech Nation is an *industry* endorser, not an academic one. Frame it as sector contribution first, research second |

**He plausibly satisfies mandatory + 2, 3 and arguably 2 more.** That is above the minimum,
which is the right place to be.

### 5.5 Exact document rules (GOV.UK, current)

These are prescriptive and applications are refused on format:

- **CV** — typed, **maximum 3 sides of A4**, career history and publications
- **Personal statement** — required; must be **forward-looking** (what he will contribute to
  the UK), not a retrospective career summary. This is one of the most common refusal causes
- **3 letters of recommendation**, mandatory. Each author must be an **established expert in
  digital technology** who has **known his work for 12+ months**. Each letter **up to 3
  single sides of A4** excluding the author's credentials and contact details; typed, dated,
  signed; the author must supply a CV or credentials plus contact and organisation details
- **Evidence portfolio — maximum 10 documents**, each **up to 3 sides of A4**:
  - **at least 2** documents for the **mandatory** criterion
  - **at least 4** documents covering **two optional** criteria (**2 per criterion**)
  - **the same evidence cannot be used for more than one criterion** — this trips people up
  - the CV and the recommendation letters **do not count** toward the 10

**Practical consequence: the three letters are the long pole.** Securing genuinely specific,
non-generic letters from three senior figures who have known his work for a year takes
**6–10 weeks** of asking, drafting and iterating. Tech Nation recommends preparing 4–5 and
using the best 3. **This is the item to start first, because it depends on other people.**

### 5.6 Realistic odds and timeline

- **The endorsement is the gate, not the visa.** Secondary reporting (immigration firms,
  which market services and should be read with that in mind) puts digital-technology Stage 1
  refusals at roughly **20–35%**, against a blended ~72% endorsement success across all
  Global Talent fields; and states that once endorsed, the visa is granted at close to 99%.
  **Treat those figures as indicative only** — I could not locate a Home Office statistical
  release breaking endorsement outcomes down by endorsing body, and any single-body rate
  should be treated as unverified.
- **Most-cited refusal reasons**: generic recommendation letters that could describe anyone;
  achievements listed without measurable impact; retrospective rather than forward-looking
  personal statements; evidence that does not clearly map to the stated criterion.
- **Realistic end-to-end**: 6–10 weeks preparation (letters dominate) + 5–8 weeks endorsement
  + 3 weeks visa ≈ **4–5 months**. Tech Nation itself suggests allowing up to 12 weeks of
  preparation.
- Secondary sources report an **expedited ~3-week endorsement target for AI and
  cybersecurity applications** introduced in 2025. **Unverified against GOV.UK — check, and
  if real, apply under an AI framing.**

### 5.7 UK Skilled Worker — the fallback

- **General salary threshold £41,700** (or the occupation's going rate, whichever is higher),
  effective for certificates of sponsorship issued from **22 July 2025**.
- The **skill level was raised to RQF 6 (degree level)** in the same reform. Software and AI
  engineering occupations are classified "higher skilled" and remain eligible; "medium
  skilled" occupations now need to be on the Immigration Salary List or Temporary Shortage
  List. **He is unaffected by the tightening** — his occupation stayed in scope.
- Requires a **licensed sponsor**. See §6.1.

Skilled Worker is a perfectly good route if a UK employer wants him. Global Talent is
better because it does not depend on anyone wanting him first.

## 6. How sponsorship actually works when nobody advertises it

This is the section that changes his day-to-day behaviour.

### 6.1 The core insight: most of Europe has no sponsorship regime at all

Prior research found **one hard sponsorship signal across 3,782 live European postings.**
The natural inference — *"almost nobody sponsors"* — is wrong. The correct inference is
**most European employers have nothing to advertise.**

| Country | Employer licence required? | Public register? | What "sponsorship" actually means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **UK** | **Yes** — Home Office sponsor licence | **Yes** — Register of Licensed Sponsors (Workers) | A real, binding gate. Unlicensed employer = impossible |
| **Netherlands** | **Yes** — IND recognised sponsor | **Yes** — IND public register | A real, binding gate |
| **Denmark** | **Only for the Fast-track scheme** — SIRI certification | **Yes** — list of certified companies | Gate for fast-track only; other Danish routes are open to any employer |
| **Germany** | **No** | No | Any lawfully trading employer can hire a Blue Card holder. There is nothing to be "licensed" for |
| **Ireland** | **No** | No | Any Revenue/CRO-registered employer can file a CSEP. Trusted Partner is obsolete as a filter |
| **Sweden, France, Spain, Portugal, Poland, Belgium** | **No** | No | Same — the employer files a form |

So in Germany, Ireland, Sweden and the southern EU, **an employer that has never sponsored
anyone is still fully able to hire him.** They simply have no reason to mention immigration
in a job ad, because from their side it is a post-offer HR task, not a hiring criterion.
Silence in a German job posting is **not** a signal of unwillingness — it is the default.

**What he should actually infer from silence:**

- **UK / NL / DK (fast-track)** — silence is uninformative; **check the register**. That is
  a hard yes/no.
- **Everywhere else** — silence is the norm. The real questions are *"has this employer done
  the paperwork before?"* and *"is HR capable of it?"* Neither is answerable from the posting.
  Both are answerable from the employee base (§6.5).

### 6.2 The registers, and exactly how to use them

**① UK — Register of Licensed Sponsors (Workers)** — GOV.UK
`gov.uk/government/publications/register-of-licensed-sponsors-workers`

- Published as a **single CSV (~10 MB)**, updated **several times per week**, sometimes daily.
- Columns: **Organisation Name, Town/City, County, Type & Rating, Route**.
- **How to use it:**
  1. Download the CSV.
  2. Filter `Route` = **Skilled Worker**. (Global Talent needs no sponsor, so this filter is
     for the fallback route only.)
  3. Filter `Type & Rating` = **A (Premium)** or **A (SMS)**. A **B rating** means the sponsor
     is on an action plan — a live compliance risk. Avoid.
  4. Filter `Town/City` to London, Cambridge, Bristol, Edinburgh, Oxford.
  5. **Left-join it against his target-company list** from `market-demand-2026.md`. A match
     means "legally able." No match means "cannot hire you on a Skilled Worker visa,
     regardless of how well the interview goes."
  6. Search the register *first* by keyword — "robotics", "vision", "AI", "imaging" — to
     surface licensed employers he has not yet heard of. **This is the register's most
     under-used function: it is a discovery tool, not just a validation tool.** Roughly
     130,000 organisations hold licences; a large number of small British computer-vision
     and robotics firms are on it and will never appear in a job-board search.

**② Netherlands — IND public register of recognised sponsors** — `ind.nl`
`ind.nl/en/public-register-recognised-sponsors` → *Labour regular and highly skilled migrants*

- Fields: **organisation name + KVK (Chamber of Commerce) number**.
- **Updated monthly** — most recent observed update **3 August 2026**.
- Thousands of organisations (a third-party mirror claims 12,000+; treat that count as
  unverified, but the order of magnitude is right).
- **How to use it:** exactly as the UK register. **Not listed = do not apply.** A non-recognised
  Dutch employer must first apply for recognition itself — a months-long process with a
  four-figure fee — which no company will do for a mid-level candidate. This is the single
  most binary filter in the entire European job search.
- **The KVK number is the useful bit.** Company names in the register are legal entities
  (`Foo Holding B.V.`), not trading names. Match on KVK, not on the brand name, or he will
  generate false negatives.

**③ Denmark — SIRI list of certified companies** — `nyidanmark.dk` → *Words and concepts* →
*SIRI* → *Certified companies*

- **Name + CVR number**, over 1,000 entities. **Last updated 28 July 2026.**
- Certification enables the **Fast-track scheme** (1-month processing). Non-certified Danish
  employers can still hire him via the Positive List or Pay Limit scheme — it is just slower.
  So here, absence from the list is a **speed** signal, not a **possibility** signal.

**④ Ireland — no register, and beware the obsolete advice.** The Trusted Partner Initiative
has been absorbed into EPOS; DETE states there is no longer a requirement for a separate
Trusted Partner registration. **Any Irish employer can file.** Filtering Irish targets by
"Trusted Partner status" is a waste of time and would wrongly exclude good employers.

**⑤ Germany, Sweden, France, Spain, Portugal, Poland, Belgium — no register exists.**
Anyone claiming to sell "the list of German companies that sponsor visas" is selling a
scraped LinkedIn export. There is no such official list because there is no such official
category.

### 6.3 What to do where no register exists

Substitute **behavioural evidence** for the missing register. Signals that an employer has
done this before, in rough order of reliability:

1. **Existing non-EU nationals in the engineering team.** LinkedIn people-search on the
   company, filtered by past locations in India, Taiwan, China, Brazil, Turkey, Iran,
   Nigeria. If a Munich company has engineers whose previous role was in Bangalore or
   Taipei, **it has processed a Blue Card**. This is the strongest available proxy and it
   takes five minutes per company.
2. **English as the working language.** Stated on the careers page or evident from
   German-company job ads written in English. An English-language ad from a German employer
   is an implicit statement that they expect non-German applicants.
3. **Size and funding.** >50 engineers, or Series B+. Below that, HR capacity to run a visa
   process is often genuinely absent — not unwillingness, incapacity.
4. **A named People Ops / HR function** rather than the founder handling hiring.
5. **Relocation package mentioned** in any posting, present or historical.
6. **Careers page has an FAQ on visas or relocation** — even a bland one. It means someone
   has asked before.

Employer types that reliably do this across Europe, in descending order of reliability:
**(a)** US tech with European engineering centres — Dublin, Zurich, Munich, London, Warsaw:
they run immigration as a standing internal function; **(b)** European deep-tech scaleups
with international investors — NavVis (Munich), Helsing, PhysicsX (London), Graphcore:
international hiring is their explicit strategy; **(c)** large European industrials with
R&D units — Bosch, Siemens, ASML, Philips, Zeiss: slow, bureaucratic, but completely
routine; **(d)** research institutes — Fraunhofer, Max Planck, INRIA, IMEC: expert at
immigration paperwork, less competitive on salary but usually clear the shortage-occupation
threshold. **Least reliable: seed-stage startups anywhere**, regardless of enthusiasm.

### 6.4 A correction to prior research

`european-market.md` records **Sword Health (Porto)** as barring relocation and requiring an
existing EU visa. That should be read as **an employer policy, not a legal constraint**.
Portugal's Blue Card threshold (~€21,030) is the lowest in Western Europe and Portugal
applies no labour market test — the country would admit him easily. Sword has simply chosen
not to do immigration paperwork. **The lesson generalises: the binding constraint in
continental Europe is usually corporate policy, not immigration law.** Which means it is
occasionally negotiable — and it means "no visa support" from one Portuguese employer says
nothing about the next one.

### 6.5 How the conversation actually goes

Given the above, the tactically correct posture differs by country:

- **UK, pre-endorsement:** say nothing about immigration; if asked, "I'm applying for a
  Global Talent visa, which doesn't require sponsorship." **Post-endorsement:** lead with it.
- **Germany/Ireland/Sweden:** never use the word "sponsorship" — it is an
  American/British-ism that makes German HR think of a cost they have not budgeted. Say
  instead: *"I'd need an EU Blue Card. I hold an MSc with a ZAB Statement of Comparability,
  my occupation is on the shortage list, and there's a statutory 4-week fast-track the
  company can use. The paperwork is on my side."* This is accurate, it is specific, and it
  signals he has already removed the work from their plate.
- **Netherlands:** check the register before applying. If they are recognised, say nothing —
  they already know their own process.
