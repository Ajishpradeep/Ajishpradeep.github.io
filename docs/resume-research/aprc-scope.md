# Taiwan APRC — what it actually unlocks, and what it does not

**Subject:** Indian citizen, holder of a Taiwan APRC (Alien Permanent Resident
Certificate). MSc, National Taipei University of Technology (EECS), 2021–2023.
AI Research Engineer in Taipei since 2023, 3D computer vision. ~8 years total
professional engineering experience.

**Research date:** 2026-08-16. Every rule below is dated. Taiwan amended both the
Immigration Act and the Foreign Professionals Act between 2023 and 2026, so
anything you read from before 2024 — including most blog posts — is likely to be
describing a stricter regime than the one now in force.

**Scope note:** this file answers "what does the APRC do." It deliberately does
*not* cover EU relocation routes (Blue Card, national permits) beyond a few
sentences — a later brief covers those. It also does not cover working remotely
from Taiwan for a European employer; that scenario is out of scope.

**Correction carried forward:** `european-market.md` §2.2 says "currently on a
Taiwan work permit." That is wrong and the wording should be deleted, not
patched. See §7 for what to write instead.

---

## Bottom line, in four sentences

1. The APRC gives him **completely open work rights in Taiwan with no work permit
   at all** — not an "own-name permit," genuinely none — plus indefinite
   residence and free re-entry.
2. The absence rule almost certainly **does not bite him**: as a foreign
   professional he falls under a carve-out where the APRC is only revoked after
   **five consecutive years without setting foot in Taiwan**. One visit resets
   it. This is the single most important finding in this brief, and it is also
   the one he must confirm in writing with the NIA.
3. Outside Taiwan the APRC is **worth essentially nothing** — not for visas, not
   for the ABTC, not for immigration points anywhere. Travel follows the
   passport.
4. **Keep "APRC" off the European CV.** It answers a question no European
   employer asked, and the honest answer to the question they *did* ask is
   "yes, you'd need to sponsor me." Exact wording in §7.

## 1. What the APRC precisely confers

### 1.1 Work rights — fully open, no permit whatsoever

There are two regimes, and it matters which one he is in.

**The regime that applies to him — Foreign Professionals Act Article 7 ¶2.**
Verbatim (`外國專業人才延攬及僱用法` Art. 7 ¶2, current text as amended
2025-09-24):

> 外國專業人才、外國特定專業人才及外國高級專業人才，其本人、配偶、未成年子女及因身心障礙無法自理生活之成年子女，**經內政部移民署許可永久居留者，在我國從事工作，不須向勞動部或教育部申請許可。**

Translation: a foreign professional (or specialist / senior professional), and
their spouse and dependent children, who has been granted permanent residence by
the NIA, **need not apply to the Ministry of Labor or the Ministry of Education
for permission to work in Taiwan.** No permit. No application. No fee.

*Source:* [Laws & Regulations Database of the ROC — Act for the Recruitment and
Employment of Foreign Professionals, Art. 7](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030295&flno=7).
Primary. Amendment date on the Act: 2025-09-24.

**The fallback regime — Employment Service Act Article 51.** For an APRC holder
who is *not* a "foreign professional" (e.g. permanent residence obtained through
a non-professional route), ESA Art. 51 ¶1(4) covers 經取得永久居留者 (those who
have obtained permanent residence) and ¶2 provides they 得不經雇主申請，逕向中央主管機關申請許可 —
they may apply **directly to the central competent authority without an
employer**. That is an own-name, portable work permit rather than an exemption.
Taiwan's Workforce Development Agency documents the process: application form,
passport copy, both sides of the APRC, **NT$100 fee, issued within one hour** in
person at the Transnational Labor Affairs Center in Taipei, or by post/online.

*Sources:* [ESA Art. 51](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=N0090001&flno=51)
(primary); [WDA, 永久居留外國人申辦個人工作許可](https://www.wda.gov.tw/News_Content.aspx?n=7F220D7E656BE749&sms=E9F640ECE968A7E1&s=551FA0C441102BB5),
published 2021-12-16 (primary, but predates the FPA Art. 7 ¶2 exemption and does
not mention it — treat as the fallback path, not the current position for
professionals).

**Either way, the practical answer to every sub-question is yes:**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Work for any employer without sponsorship? | **Yes.** No employer application, no employer tie. He can resign on Friday and start elsewhere on Monday with zero immigration paperwork. |
| Change jobs / hold multiple jobs? | **Yes.** ESA Art. 51 ¶1 lifts the Art. 52 permit-duration limits and Art. 53(3)(4) job-change restrictions; Art. 7 FPA removes the permit entirely. |
| Freelance / contract / consult? | **Yes.** With no MOL permission requirement, the ESA Art. 43 prohibition on unpermitted work has nothing to bite on. |
| Self-employed / sole proprietorship (獨資商號)? | **Yes** — sole proprietorship registration is open to APRC holders and ROC nationals, and closed to ordinary ARC holders. This is a real, APRC-specific unlock. |
| Found a company (有限公司 / 股份有限公司)? | **Yes**, and he can be sole shareholder and director; Taiwan permits 100% foreign ownership in most sectors. **But verify** whether he still needs Investment Review (投審會 / MOEA Department of Investment Review) clearance as a foreign investor — the sources conflict and none of the ones I found are primary. See §8. |
| Restricted job categories? | **Lifted.** ESA Art. 51 ¶1 disapplies Art. 46 ¶1 and ¶3 (the closed list of permitted occupations for foreigners) and Art. 47 (the domestic labour-market test / advertising requirement). |
| Employment stability fee (就業安定費)? | **Exempt** (ESA Art. 51 ¶1, disapplying Art. 55). This is a direct cost saving to an employer versus hiring a permit-holder. |

> **The commercially meaningful consequence:** a Taiwanese startup, a research
> lab, a small studio, or a foreign company with no Taiwan entity can hire him,
> or contract him, without any of the cost, delay, salary floors, or
> qualification tests that make hiring a foreigner in Taiwan otherwise painful.
> He is, to a Taiwanese hiring manager, indistinguishable from a local hire on
> paperwork. That is the part of the APRC with actual market value.

### 1.2 Residence and re-entry

Permanent residence is **indefinite** — there is no expiry date on the residence
right and no renewal cycle of the kind an ARC has. It ends only on one of the
grounds in Immigration Act Art. 32 (fraud, forged documents) or Art. 33 (see §2).

Re-entry is free and unconditional. Verbatim, Regulations Governing Visiting,
Residency, and Permanent Residency of Aliens, Art. 20:

> "Any foreign national permitted for permanent residency may re-enter the ROC
> with an Alien Permanent Resident Certificate and a valid passport."

No re-entry permit, no visa, no exit clearance, unlimited trips.
*Source:* [Regulations Governing Visiting, Residency, and Permanent Residency of
Aliens](https://law.moj.gov.tw/ENG/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=D0080129), amended
2025-12-30. Primary.

He also has National Health Insurance eligibility on the same terms as any
resident. (Flag: NHI coverage is generally suspended during prolonged absence
abroad — verify the current threshold with the NHIA before any long trip; I did
not confirm it from a primary source.)

### 1.3 What still separates him from a citizen

He has no **household registration** (戶籍), and in Taiwan almost every
citizen-only right is keyed to household registration rather than to nationality
in the abstract. Consequences:

- **No National ID card (身分證).** This is the practical one. A surprising
  amount of Taiwanese civic and commercial infrastructure — some online
  government services, some banking and brokerage products, some e-signature and
  real-name verification flows — is built around the ID number format. The APRC
  now carries a UI number in the same format, which has fixed much of this, but
  not all of it.
- **No vote, in any election, at any level, and no right to stand for office.**
- **No public office or civil service employment.**
- **No ROC passport**, and therefore no access to Taiwan's very strong visa-free
  network. See §4 — this is the crux of why the APRC does nothing for travel.
- **No conscription** (he is a foreign national; not a loss).
- **Regulated professions** — practising law, medicine, accountancy, architecture
  and similar licensed professions have their own nationality and licensing
  statutes that sit outside the Employment Service Act. Irrelevant to a software
  or research engineer, but worth knowing the open work right is not literally
  unlimited.
- **Land ownership** is governed by reciprocity, not residency. Land Act Art. 18:
  a foreigner may acquire land rights in the ROC only where ROC nationals are
  permitted the same in that foreigner's country. Good news: **India appears as
  entry 54 on List I ("Fully Reciprocal Nations") of the MOI's published list of
  reciprocal nations** for land acquisition, so no reciprocity certificate is
  required from him. But note this follows his *Indian nationality*, not his
  APRC — an APRC does not improve it, and an Indian citizen on an ARC has the
  same right.
  *Source:* MOI's "List of reciprocal nations for foreigners acquiring land in
  Taiwan, R.O.C.", republished by [Taipei City Department of Land
  Administration](https://english.land.gov.taipei/News_Content.aspx?n=7F45709855E1D2C3&s=FEAC19AF1C1F1A56&sms=5B794C46F3CDE718)
  and Taoyuan City. The PDF is not dated on its face — verify against the current
  MOI list before relying on it for a transaction.

## 2. ⚠️ THE ABSENCE RULE — the decision-relevant fact

This is where most of the internet is wrong, including sources published as
recently as 2026.

### 2.1 The general rule (which probably does *not* apply to him)

Immigration Act (入出國及移民法) Article 33, subparagraph 4, verbatim:

> 四、永久居留期間，**最近五年平均每年居住未達一百八十三日。**但因出國就學、就醫或**其他特殊原因經移民署同意者，不在此限。**

Translation: the NIA **shall revoke** permanent residence and cancel the APRC
where, during the period of permanent residence, the holder **has resided in
Taiwan for less than 183 days per year on average over the most recent five
years**. Exempted where the person has NIA approval for study abroad, medical
treatment, **or other special reasons**.

Three details that matter and that the blogs get wrong:

1. **It is a rolling five-year average, not a per-year test.** 183 × 5 = **915
   days across any rolling five-year window**. He can be away for one entire
   year, or two, and still comply if other years over-compensate. The old rule
   was 183 days *each and every* year; it was relaxed in the 2023 Immigration Act
   amendment. Any source telling him "you must stay 183 days every year or you
   lose it" is describing the pre-2024 law.
2. **The clock starts on 1 January of the year *following* the issue of the
   APRC** — NIA's own guidance. So the year of issue is not counted.
3. Revocation is not the end of the world: NIA guidance states that a person
   whose APRC is cancelled under Art. 33(4) but who still has residence
   eligibility **may apply for ordinary residence (an ARC) within 30 days of
   cancellation**. That preserves his legal presence but not his permanent
   status — the five-year qualifying clock for a new APRC would restart.

*Sources:* [Immigration Act Art. 33, Chinese text](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=D0080132&flno=33)
(primary; Act amendment date 2023-06-28, database current to 2026-08-07);
[NIA English "0903 Guidelines for Foreign Nationals Applying for Permanent
Residency"](https://www.immigration.gov.tw/5475/5478/141465/141808/411648/cp_news),
page updated **2026-04-21** (primary); [NIA Chinese 外國人申請永久居留送件須知](https://www.immigration.gov.tw/5385/7244/7250/7317/%E6%B0%B8%E4%B9%85%E5%B1%85%E7%95%99/362049/),
page updated **2026-01-23** (primary).

### 2.2 The carve-out that almost certainly applies to him

Both NIA pages carry the same proviso. The Chinese page states it as:

> 「…但**依人才專法第二十一條規定**或經移民署同意者，不在此限。」

— the 183-day-average rule **does not apply** to persons covered by Article 21 of
the Foreign Professionals Act (人才專法), or to persons with NIA consent.

Foreign Professionals Act Article 21, verbatim:

> 外國專業人才、外國特定專業人才及外國高級專業人才，其本人、配偶、未成年子女及因身心障礙無法自理生活之成年子女，經內政部移民署許可永久居留後，**出國五年以上未曾入國者**，內政部移民署得廢止其永久居留許可及註銷其外僑永久居留證。

Translation: for foreign professionals (and specialist / senior professionals),
plus spouse and dependent children, who have been granted permanent residence,
the NIA **may** revoke permanent residence and cancel the APRC where they have
**been outside the country for five years or more without having entered it at
all**.

*Source:* [Foreign Professionals Act Art. 21](https://law.moj.gov.tw/LawClass/LawSingle.aspx?pcode=A0030295&flno=21).
Primary. Act amendment date 2025-09-24.

Read the wording carefully — it is remarkably generous:

- The trigger is **五年以上未曾入國** — five or more years *without a single
  entry*. It is not a day-count. It is not an average. **A single entry to Taiwan
  resets the clock completely.**
- The verb is **得** ("may"), not 應 ("shall") — even at five years, revocation is
  discretionary, not automatic. Contrast Art. 33 of the Immigration Act, which
  uses 撤銷或廢止 as a mandatory instruction.
- It covers 本人、配偶、未成年子女 — him, a spouse, and minor children.

> ### What this means in practice
>
> **If he is treated as a 外國專業人才 (foreign professional), relocating abroad
> does not cost him the APRC, provided he visits Taiwan at least once in any
> five-year period.** A single week-long trip every four years is sufficient
> compliance. He could move to Berlin, work there for a decade, fly to Taipei
> twice over that decade, and keep permanent residency the entire time.
>
> That converts the APRC from a status that ties him to Taiwan into a **standing
> option to return** — which is a materially different and much more valuable
> thing. He does not have to choose between Europe and Taiwan.

### 2.3 The one thing he must verify himself

The carve-out turns on whether the NIA classifies **him** as a 外國專業人才.

The definition in FPA Art. 4 is broad — a foreign national who may engage in
"professional work" in Taiwan, where "professional work" cross-references
Employment Service Act Art. 46 ¶1(1)–(6), the first limb of which is specialised
or technical work. An AI research engineer plainly falls inside it, and the
statutory text of Art. 21 keys off *being* a foreign professional who obtained
permanent residence, not off having obtained the APRC via the FPA's own Art. 18
route rather than Immigration Act Art. 25.

**But this is an administrative determination made by the NIA, not something a
statute settles for him.** The gap between "the text says X" and "the officer at
the counter in year six applies X" is exactly where permanent-residency cases go
wrong, and by the time it matters he will have been abroad for years and will
have very little leverage.

**Action:** before any relocation, get written confirmation from the NIA that
Art. 21 of the Foreign Professionals Act applies to his APRC. Ask in writing (NIA
service centre or the 1990 foreign-national hotline; ask for a written reply, not
a phone answer), naming his APRC number and the legal basis on which it was
issued. Keep the reply. Also keep documentary proof of the professional character
of his employment (contract, title, job description) — that is the evidence the
classification will rest on.

**Belt and braces:** the Art. 33 proviso also allows NIA consent for "study
abroad, medical treatment, or other special reasons." A secondary source claims
one can apply for "preservation of APRC" before departing, extending permissible
absence to ten years. **I could not find any primary basis for the ten-year
figure** — the Regulations Governing Visiting, Residency, and Permanent Residency
of Aliens (amended 2025-12-30) contain no procedure, form, or time limit for this
consent at all. Treat it as undocumented NIA discretion. If he is going abroad
for a qualifying reason, apply for the consent *before* leaving and get it in
writing; do not rely on the ten-year claim.

### 2.4 Where the sources conflict

The [Taiwan Employment Gold Card office's APRC page](https://goldcard.nat.gov.tw/en/tags/aprc/)
states that "left the State for more than five years without re-entering" is a
ground for revocation **under Article 33 of the Immigration Act**. That is
wrong — Art. 33 contains no such provision; the five-year rule lives in Art. 21
of the Foreign Professionals Act, a separate statute operating as *lex
specialis*. The Gold Card office is an official body, but on this point the
statute and the NIA's own guidance pages take primacy. The distinction matters
because Art. 21 only helps him if he is inside its personal scope — if the rule
really lived in Art. 33 it would apply to everybody, and it does not.

## 3. Naturalisation

### 3.1 The standard route

Nationality Act (國籍法) Art. 3 — an alien may apply to naturalise if he:

1. has **legally resided in the ROC for more than 183 days each year for at least
   five consecutive years**;
2. has legal capacity under both ROC and home-country law;
3. has no criminal record (police clearance);
4. "possesses enough property or professional skills to support himself/herself
   or lead a stable life"; and
5. has **basic proficiency in the national language and basic knowledge of the
   rights and obligations of ROC nationals**.

*Source:* [Nationality Act](https://law.moj.gov.tw/ENG/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=D0030001),
amendment date 2024-05-24. Primary.

On (1): he has been resident since 2021, so on a plain reading he is at or near
eligible now. Note the residence test for *naturalisation* is still the stricter
"183 days **each** year," not the five-year average used for keeping the APRC.

On (5): **he is very likely exempt from the test.** Under the Standards for
Identification of Basic Language Abilities and General Knowledge of the Rights
and Duties of Naturalized R.O.C. Citizens, applicants who have **studied at a
public or registered private school in Taiwan for more than one year** do not
need to sit the exam. A two-year MSc at NTUT should satisfy this. Otherwise the
route is 200 hours of recognised classes or a 20-question oral or written exam at
70% (for Art. 3 applicants). Verify with the MOI / his household registration
office that a Mandarin-taught-or-not master's programme counts — the standard
refers to study at the school, not to the language of instruction, but confirm.

*Source:* [Standards for Identification of Basic Language Abilities…](https://law.moj.gov.tw/ENG/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=D0030028).
Primary.

### 3.2 The renunciation requirement, and the high-level-professional exemption

Nationality Act Art. 9: a naturalising applicant must **provide a certificate of
loss of original nationality within one year** of naturalisation being approved.

Exemptions exist, and one of them is real and relevant: applicants recognised as
**high-level professionals (高級專業人才)**, on the recommendation of the relevant
central competent authority, may naturalise **without renouncing** their original
nationality. This was introduced by the 2016 amendment.

The criteria live in a separate instrument, the **Standards for Defining
High-Level Professionals for Naturalization**
([law.moj.gov.tw pcode=D0030033](https://law.moj.gov.tw/ENG/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=D0030033),
amendment date **2024-10-17**). The categories that would be his:

- **Technology, §1(1):** unique talents or outstanding R&D capability in
  information and communication technology, including cybersecurity.
- **Technology, §1(2):** "forward-looking technologies such as **AI**, IoT,
  blockchain, VR, robots, and additive manufacturing."
- **Economy, §2(2):** outstanding professional talent or transnational experience
  in **semiconductors, IC, optoelectronics, information and communication**
  sectors meeting Taiwan's urgent needs.

Process: obtain a **"Recommendation for Naturalization for High-Level
Professionals"** from the relevant central competent authority (for his field,
realistically the NSTC or MOEA), valid **six months**, then a review committee
convened by the Ministry of the Interior decides.

Notably, the Standards contain **no salary threshold, patent count, or award
requirement** — the assessment is discretionary and rests on the ministry's
recommendation.

### 3.3 How realistic is it for him — honest assessment

**Not very, yet, and possibly pointless anyway.**

**On volume:** per an MOI survey reported through MOFA, **330 people were
naturalised as high-level professionals without renouncing their original
nationality across 2016–2024** — roughly 37 a year, from a foreign resident
population in the hundreds of thousands. The largest cohort, **123, was in
academic research and education**; 65 in business. This is an elite,
discretionary, ministry-nominated channel. It goes to professors, principal
investigators, named-award holders and senior executives — not, typically, to a
mid-career industry engineer with a master's, however good.
*(Source: MOI statistics via MOFA's New Southbound Policy Portal. I was unable to
load the page directly on 2026-08-16 — 503 — so this figure is reported from the
search index and should be re-verified against MOI before being relied on.)*

To become a plausible candidate he would need to spend several years
deliberately building the dossier: granted patents in his own name, first-author
publications at top-tier venues, a principal/staff-level title, involvement in an
NSTC- or MOEA-backed programme, and a sponsor inside a ministry willing to sign
the recommendation. That is a 3–5 year project undertaken *for its own sake*, not
a box to tick.

**On whether the exemption even helps him — the point everyone misses:**
Taiwan's willingness to let him keep his original nationality is close to
irrelevant, because **India will not.** Section 9 of India's Citizenship Act 1955
provides that an Indian citizen who *voluntarily acquires* the citizenship of
another country **automatically ceases** to be an Indian citizen — the Supreme
Court has confirmed this is an automatic legal consequence, not a discretionary
administrative act. India does not permit dual citizenship. So if he naturalises
as ROC, he loses Indian citizenship by operation of Indian law regardless of what
Taiwan permits, and would fall back to OCI (Overseas Citizen of India) status,
which is not citizenship.

**Therefore:** the high-level-professional route is worth understanding as a
signal of how Taiwan values his field, and worth revisiting if he ever decides to
settle in Taiwan permanently — but it should not be treated as a live option in a
career-planning decision made now, and its headline benefit (keeping the original
passport) is largely nullified by Indian law. He should confirm the Indian
position with the MHA / the Indian mission before taking any irreversible step.

One further note: Nationality Act Art. 10 bars naturalised citizens from certain
public offices for ten years after naturalisation. Not relevant to an engineer,
but it is a real residual difference from birthright citizenship.

## 4. Travel and mobility — where it helps and where it does nothing

**The general principle is correct and holds almost everywhere: visa access
follows the passport, not the residence permit.** His Indian passport determines
his visa requirements. The APRC changes nothing about that.

Specifically:

| Scheme | Does the APRC help? |
|---|---|
| **Visa-free / visa-on-arrival anywhere** | **No.** He gets exactly what an Indian passport gets, from Taiwan or from India. |
| **Schengen visa waiver** | **No.** Schengen exemption is nationality-based. Taiwanese *passport* holders travel visa-free to Schengen; APRC holders do not — the German Institute Taipei's own page is explicit that "nationals from other countries residing in Taiwan might require a visa to enter Germany." |
| **ETIAS** | Irrelevant — ETIAS applies to visa-exempt nationalities; he is not one. |
| **APEC Business Travel Card** | **No, twice over.** Taiwan's BOCA requires a valid ROC passport containing a national ID number, which he cannot have. And separately, the ABTC is a citizens' scheme: **Hong Kong is the only APEC economy that admits permanent residents**, and **India is not an APEC member economy at all**. |
| **Third-country "residence permit shortcut" schemes** | **No.** The Philippines admits Indian nationals visa-free if they hold a valid **American, Japanese, Australian, Canadian, Schengen, Singaporean or UK** visa or residence permit — the "AJACSSUK" list. The UAE's expanded visa-on-arrival for Indians keys off US green cards and UK/EU/Singapore/Japan/Korea/Australia/NZ/Canada residence. **Taiwan is on none of these lists.** |
| **Re-entry to Taiwan** | **Yes** — unlimited, no visa, no re-entry permit (Regulations Governing Visiting, Residency, and Permanent Residency of Aliens, Art. 20). |

### The one place it genuinely helps

**Jurisdiction and evidence for visa applications filed in Taipei.** He applies at
the relevant European representative office in Taipei rather than through India's
mission network. Two concrete advantages:

1. **Access.** The German Institute Taipei's EU Blue Card visa checklist (page
   dated **02.06.2025**) explicitly lists, under required documents: *"for non
   Taiwanese: Taiwanese ARC/APRC, in original plus 1 copy."* Third-country
   nationals resident in Taiwan can and do file there. He is not competing for
   appointment slots in the Indian consular queue, which is a materially
   different experience.
2. **Evidence of ties.** Short-stay Schengen refusals for Indian nationals turn
   heavily on "intention to return" / ties to country of residence. **Permanent
   residence plus stable long-term employment in Taiwan is about as strong a
   ties document as a non-citizen can produce.** This does not change the legal
   test; it changes the risk assessment in his favour, and it is a real
   advantage — just not a legal right, and not a guarantee.

*Sources:* [German Institute Taipei — Schengen visas](https://taipei.diplo.de/tw-en/service/visa/1788242-1788242);
[German Institute Taipei — EU Blue Card checklist](https://taipei.diplo.de/tw-en/service/visa/2455584-2455584)
(dated 02.06.2025); [BOCA — APEC Business Travel Card](https://www.boca.gov.tw/cp-390-725-5cfc3-1.html);
[APEC Business Mobility Group](https://www.apec.org/groups/committee-on-trade-and-investment/business-mobility-group/faq).
All primary/official.

## 5. How the profile reads to employers and immigration systems abroad

### 5.1 To immigration systems: it counts for nothing

Be blunt about this internally, because it disciplines the CV decision. No
immigration system outside Taiwan gives credit for Taiwanese permanent residency:

- **EU Blue Card and national highly-skilled routes** — eligibility keys off a
  recognised higher-education qualification plus a salary threshold plus an
  employer. Third-country PR is not a factor. (Detail deferred to the EU
  relocation brief.)
- **Canada Express Entry** — CRS awards points for *Canadian* status, education,
  language and experience. Taiwanese PR scores zero. Foreign degrees require an
  Educational Credential Assessment.
- **United States** — H-1B is a lottery; the 20,000-place master's exemption
  applies only to degrees from **US** institutions, so his NTUT MSc does not
  qualify him for it. O-1 turns on evidence of extraordinary ability. Taiwanese
  PR is irrelevant to both.
- **Japan Highly Skilled Professional points system, Korea E-7, Singapore
  Employment Pass / COMPASS** — all score education, salary, experience, age,
  local language, and (for COMPASS) employer diversity. **None award anything for
  a third country's permanent residency.**

So: as a *legal* asset outside Taiwan, the APRC is worth zero. Its value abroad
is purely informational.

### 5.2 To a human reader: mildly positive, with one real risk

What it genuinely signals:

- **He is not on a cliff edge.** A hiring manager looking at "Indian national,
  working in Taiwan" may assume an employer-tied permit, which raises the
  question "is he applying because his visa is running out?" Permanent residency
  answers that: he is applying because he wants to, from a position of security.
  This is a quiet but real de-risking signal.
- **He has completed one international relocation successfully.** He moved from
  India to Taiwan in 2021, finished a degree in a non-English-dominant
  environment, and has held professional employment there for years — long enough
  to qualify for permanent residence. Most sponsorship hesitancy is fear of a
  failed relocation, not fear of paperwork cost. He has direct evidence against
  that fear.

The risk, and it is not small:

- **"Permanent resident of Taiwan" can read as "settled in Taiwan."** A recruiter
  scanning for relocation intent may take permanent residency as evidence that
  his life is anchored somewhere else and that he is a low-probability closer.
  Stating a status that gives him rights he is *not* proposing to use invites
  exactly the wrong inference.

### 5.3 The degree — this genuinely needs attention

The Taiwanese MSc is the credential that will need formal treatment, and it is
independent of the APRC.

- **Germany:** for a Blue Card, the German mission requires either (a) anabin
  database listings confirming **institution rated H+**, **degree type matching**,
  and **field of study matching**, or (b) a **Statement of Comparability
  (Zeugnisbewertung) from the ZAB**. All three anabin elements must be positively
  confirmed. *He must check anabin for 國立臺北科技大學 / National Taipei University
  of Technology himself* — I could not confirm its listing (anabin is a
  JavaScript application and not fetchable). If NTUT is not listed H+, apply for
  a ZAB Zeugnisbewertung; budget a few months and a modest fee, and start it
  before he needs it, not during an offer window.
- **Rest of EU:** there is **no automatic EU-wide recognition of academic
  diplomas**; each country runs its own procedure via its ENIC-NARIC centre.
  Netherlands (Nuffic), Ireland (NARIC Ireland), Nordics — an evaluation is
  usually cheap and quick, and in practice the employer or the immigration
  authority drives it.
- **UK:** a UK ENIC Statement of Comparability is the standard artefact.
- **Canada:** an ECA (WES or equivalent) is mandatory for Express Entry.

None of this is a barrier. All of it is a lead-time item. **The single most
useful administrative thing he can do this year is obtain a ZAB Statement of
Comparability (or the equivalent for his target country) and have it sitting in a
folder.** It converts an unknown-quantity credential into a stated German
master's equivalent, which is worth far more on a European application than any
mention of residency status.

## 6. Professional scope inside Taiwan and around APAC

### 6.1 Taiwan — where the open work right actually pays off

His profile — 3D computer vision, on-device/edge inference, applied AI — sits
almost exactly on top of Taiwan's comparative advantage. Taiwan is not a strong
market for frontier-model research; it is arguably the world's strongest market
for **AI that has to run on constrained silicon inside a shipping device**.

Named employers with publicly relevant work:

**Edge-AI and vision silicon (the closest fit):**
- **MediaTek** — the largest employer of edge-AI talent in Taiwan; NPU
  architecture, camera ISP and CV pipelines, on-device model optimisation. The
  most direct match for "optimise models for hardware you helped design."
- **Himax Technologies** — ultra-low-power AI vision processors (WiseEye) and
  3D sensing / structured-light modules. Unusually on-point for 3D CV
  specifically.
- **Novatek Microelectronics** — image and display SoCs, surveillance imaging.
- **Realtek Semiconductor** — connectivity and multimedia SoCs with AI blocks.
- **eYs3D Microsystems** — stereo depth-camera modules and depth SoCs. A small
  company, but one of the few Taiwanese firms whose core product *is* 3D vision.
- Also active in AI silicon: Elan Microelectronics, Sunplus, Egis Technology.

**Systems, robotics and industrial vision:**
- **Delta Electronics** — machine vision, industrial robotics, smart
  manufacturing.
- **Advantech** — industrial edge-AI vision platforms.
- **Foxconn / Hon Hai** — EV perception, factory robotics, and the NVIDIA
  partnership building an AI-factory supercomputer in Taiwan (announced 2025).
- **Quanta, Wistron, Pegatron, Inventec** — AI server and edge-device
  engineering; increasingly building software teams rather than only ODM
  hardware.
- **TSMC** — computer vision applied to defect inspection and metrology, a large
  and underappreciated internal CV practice.

**XR and spatial computing (direct 3D-CV relevance):**
- **HTC / VIVE**, **XRSPACE** — 6DoF tracking, SLAM, hand and body tracking.
- **AUO**, **Coretronic** — display and optical engines with 3D sensing work.

**Research institutions and multinationals:**
- **ITRI (Industrial Technology Research Institute)** and **Academia Sinica** —
  applied and academic research posts respectively.
- **NVIDIA Taiwan** — significantly expanded Taipei presence, including a new
  Taiwan headquarters announced in 2025.
- **Google Taiwan** — Google's largest R&D site outside the United States.
- **Microsoft** — Taiwan AI R&D hub.

*Caveat: I have verified that these companies operate in these sectors, not that
each currently has an open 3D-CV headcount. Treat the list as a map of where the
work lives, not a vacancy list.*

**The specific advantage the APRC confers in this market** is optionality that
his peers do not have. Foreign engineers on employer-tied permits in Taiwan are
structurally disadvantaged: they cannot easily join a startup that lacks the
paid-in capital or revenue history to sponsor, cannot contract, cannot take a
fractional or advisory role, and negotiate under the implicit threat that leaving
means leaving the country. He has none of those constraints. He can join a
five-person spin-out, or consult for three companies at once, or found something,
with no immigration consequence whatsoever. In a market where the interesting
edge-AI work is increasingly happening at small, fast, undercapitalised
companies, that is the real prize.

### 6.2 APAC — the honest answer

**The APRC confers no rights in Japan, Korea or Singapore. None.** Each requires
its own work visa, obtained on its own terms, and none of their points systems
credit a third country's permanent residency (see §5.1).

What *does* travel is the substance of the CV, and it travels well:

- **Japan** — device makers (Sony, Canon, Panasonic, Denso, Honda, Preferred
  Networks) hire heavily for on-device perception and 3D vision. The Highly
  Skilled Professional points system rewards degree, salary, age and research
  output; his MSc and ~8 years' experience make the threshold reachable, and the
  Taiwan hardware-adjacent background reads as directly relevant rather than
  exotic. Japanese language ability is worth points and, more importantly, worth
  a lot in practice.
- **Korea** — Samsung, LG, Hyundai and the E-7 visa route. Similar dynamics;
  language matters more than in Taiwan.
- **Singapore** — Employment Pass with COMPASS scoring. His salary band and
  qualification should clear it; the harder variable is COMPASS's employer
  workforce-diversity criterion, which is outside his control. Singapore has a
  thinner edge-silicon ecosystem than Taiwan but a much deeper applied-AI and
  research-lab scene.

**Net:** regional employers value the profile highly — a Taiwan-based engineer who
has shipped vision models onto real silicon is a credible and somewhat scarce
hire across APAC. But every one of those moves is a fresh visa application in
which the APRC contributes nothing but a line of context.

## 7. Should any of this go on the CV? — decisive recommendation

### The recommendation: no. Keep "APRC" off the European CV entirely.

Not because it is embarrassing, and not because of European CV convention on
nationality — but because it fails the only test that matters: **does it answer a
question the reader is actually asking, in a way that helps him?**

The European reader's question is *"do I have to sponsor this person?"* For a
Berlin or Amsterdam role, the honest answer is **yes** — his Taiwanese permanent
residency does nothing for them. Volunteering an impressive-sounding status that
does not answer their question produces three bad outcomes at once:

1. **"APRC" is an unfamiliar acronym.** A European recruiter has never seen it.
   Unfamiliar acronyms in a status line read as noise, or worse, as an attempt to
   imply a work right that does not exist. If a reader has to Google a term to
   understand a claim, the claim is doing negative work.
2. **Spelled out — "Permanent Resident of Taiwan" — it actively harms him.** It
   reads as *settled elsewhere*. It hands a screener a reason to deprioritise on
   relocation-probability grounds, which is precisely the objection he needs to
   defeat.
3. **It costs a line of the most valuable real estate on the page** to convey
   information that changes no decision.

### What to write instead

**On the CV — one line in the contact block, and only for markets where a status
line is conventional (DACH, Switzerland):**

```
Indian national · MSc (EU comparability statement available) · EU Blue Card eligible
```

Delete `currently on a Taiwan work permit` from `european-market.md` §2.2. Do not
replace it with `currently holds a Taiwan APRC`. The correct move is deletion,
not substitution.

For the UK, Ireland, the Netherlands and the Nordics, where a status line is not
conventional, **omit it entirely** and let the cover letter carry the argument.

**In the cover letter — where the relocation argument belongs, framed as
evidence, never as status:**

> "I moved from India to Taiwan in 2021 to complete my MSc, and have built my
> career here since — long enough to have been granted permanent residency. I
> know what an international move costs and what it takes to make one work. I am
> looking to make a second, deliberate one, to Europe."

That sentence does everything the status line was meant to do — establishes
proven relocation capability, quietly signals that he is not fleeing a visa
problem — and it does so as a narrative claim a human will read, rather than as a
credential a screener will misparse. The word "permanent residency" earns its
place *here* because the surrounding sentence tells the reader what to conclude
from it.

### The two situations where the rule reverses

**1. Any role based in Taiwan, or a Taiwan/APAC entity of a multinational.** Here
it is not a nice-to-have, it is the single most valuable line on the page — it
removes cost, delay, salary-floor constraints and the employment stability fee
from the employer's side of the ledger. Put it at the top, unambiguously:

```
Work authorisation: Taiwan permanent resident (APRC) — no work permit or sponsorship required
```

Note the construction: the *benefit to the employer* is stated in plain English
after the dash, so the acronym never has to carry meaning on its own.

**2. LinkedIn and application-form free-text fields.** Different medium, different
rules. Recruiters filter on work-authorisation strings and application forms ask
directly. Answer the form's question honestly — "will you require sponsorship in
[country]? Yes" — and use LinkedIn's about-section, not the headline, for
anything more. A line in the LinkedIn "About" reading *"Permanent resident of
Taiwan; open to relocation to Europe"* is fine, because LinkedIn is a place where
context is expected and the reader has infinite scroll. A CV is not.

### Summary table

| Context | Mention APRC? | Wording |
|---|---|---|
| CV, Germany / Austria / Switzerland | **No** | `Indian national · MSc (EU comparability statement available) · EU Blue Card eligible` |
| CV, UK / IE / NL / Nordics | **No** | Omit status entirely |
| CV, North America | **No** | Omit; the H-1B/O-1 question is settled by other facts |
| Cover letter, Europe | **Yes, as narrative** | See paragraph above |
| CV, Taiwan or APAC-entity roles | **Yes, prominently** | `Work authorisation: Taiwan permanent resident (APRC) — no work permit or sponsorship required` |
| LinkedIn "About" | **Optional, yes** | `Permanent resident of Taiwan; open to relocation to Europe` |
| Application forms | **Answer honestly** | Answer the question asked; do not volunteer more |

## 9. Honest gaps — things he must verify himself

Ordered by how much damage getting them wrong would do.

1. **⚠️ Whether the NIA treats him as a 外國專業人才 for FPA Art. 21 purposes.**
   The entire "relocating abroad costs him nothing" conclusion rests on this. The
   statutory text supports it and the NIA's own guidance page states the
   carve-out, but classification is administrative. **Get it in writing from the
   NIA before relocating.** Also worth asking explicitly whether the carve-out
   applies where the APRC was issued under Immigration Act Art. 25 rather than
   FPA Art. 18 — I found no source that addresses the interaction directly, and
   it is the most plausible failure mode.
2. **His APRC issue date**, which fixes when the five-year window began (1
   January of the following year). He should know this number precisely.
3. **The "special reasons" consent procedure under Immigration Act Art. 33(4).**
   No form, no criteria, no time limit is published in the Regulations. Purely
   discretionary. If he needs it, apply *before* departure and get the answer in
   writing. Ignore the "10 years" figure circulating on blogs.
4. **anabin's listing for National Taipei University of Technology** — check
   anabin.kmk.org directly for institution rating (needs H+), degree type match,
   and field-of-study match. If any of the three fails, order a ZAB
   Zeugnisbewertung. Do this now, not during an offer window.
5. **Indian citizenship consequences of any ROC naturalisation** — confirm with
   MHA / the Indian mission. Section 9 operates automatically; there is no
   application and no discretion, and OCI is not a substitute for citizenship.
6. **Investment Review clearance for company formation.** Sources disagree on
   whether an APRC holder incorporating in Taiwan is treated as a foreign
   investor requiring MOEA Department of Investment Review approval. Ask a
   Taiwanese accountant before incorporating; the sole-proprietorship route
   appears cleanly open.
7. **NHI during prolonged absence** — enrolment is generally suspended after an
   extended period abroad. Confirm the current threshold and the resumption
   process with the NHIA.
8. **Tax residency on departure.** Leaving Taiwan makes him a non-resident for
   income-tax purposes (the 183-day test), which changes withholding on any
   Taiwan-source income to flat non-resident rates. Not an immigration question,
   but it lands in the same decision and is easy to overlook.
9. **The MOI high-level-professional naturalisation statistics (330, 2016–2024)**
   — reported via search indexing of a MOFA portal page that returned 503 on
   2026-08-16. Re-verify against MOI before quoting the figure anywhere it
   matters.
