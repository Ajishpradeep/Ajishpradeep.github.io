## 1. Key findings — CV conventions

### 1.1 Two pages is the European norm, and it is not a concession

ETH Application Guide 2025:

> "We recommend keeping it to two pages for bachelor and master students and up
> to three for doctoral candidates and postdocs."

Its CV checklist restates this as a hard cap: *"Number of pages — BSc, MSc
students max. 2 pages; doctoral students, post-docs max. 3 pages."* The same
guide describes the US resume as *"No longer than one page."*

So the one-page rule is a **US convention**, not a universal one. Europe's
expectation runs the other way: a two-page CV with complete month/year records is
the default, and a one-page CV from someone with eight years of history reads as
incomplete rather than disciplined.

**What this means for Pradeep specifically:** two pages, without apology, and
without the compression damage that a forced one-pager would do to his strongest
material (the deployment scale, the metric/failure-mode pairs, the external
validation). He is an MSc-holder in industry, not a doctoral candidate — three
pages is not available to him. If a research-lab role asks for a fuller output
record, ETH's own instruction is to **attach it as a separate document** rather
than inflate the CV: *"If relevant, attach a complete list as a separate
document."*

### 1.2 Personal data: the real fault line is not Europe-vs-US, it is traditional-local vs international-tech

This is where conventional wisdom is most wrong, in both directions.

**European institutions still formally expect personal data.** ETH's Welcome
Center page — written for people applying for jobs *in Switzerland* — lists as
required CV content:

> "First name and surname / address / telephone numbers / e-mail address / date
> of birth / marital status / number of children and their ages / place of origin
> (for Swiss nationals) / **nationality and residence permit (for foreign
> nationals)**"

The Application Guide's CV checklist is only slightly softer: *"personal
information — date of birth; nationality (residence permit, if applicable);
marital status (optional); photograph — business formal attire suitable to the
position."* And on photos:

> "A photograph on your CV is optional, but very common in German-speaking
> countries."

> "If you have a work permit, mention it."

The same guide flags the US contrast explicitly: *"Applications in the USA do not
include personal details such as a photograph, nationality, marital status, sex
or date of birth."*

**Denmark is more traditional than its reputation, not less.** Workindenmark
(the official Danish public employment service's international recruitment
portal):

> "It is normal to include your age, if you are married and have children, and
> what you like to do in your spare time."

It recommends placing these *at the end*, under "About me" or "Interests," and
notes Danish employers read them as fit/retention signals. This directly
contradicts the common assumption that the Nordics are the most
personal-data-averse part of Europe.

**But the counter-pressure is institutional and growing.** The
Antidiskriminierungsstelle des Bundes (German Federal Anti-Discrimination Agency)
ran a national anonymised-application pilot with Deutsche Telekom, Deutsche Post,
Procter & Gamble and others, on the finding that discrimination concentrates in
the pre-interview screening stage; it criticises the German photo norm directly
as *"Raum für Vorurteile und Diskriminierungen"* and notes that photo-bearing
applications have been internationally obsolete for years. France goes further
and legislates it: **Code du travail Art. L1221-6** restricts what may be
requested of a candidate to information having *"un lien direct et nécessaire"*
with the job, and **L1221-7** provides for applications to be examined under
conditions preserving anonymity.

**The GDPR point is widely misstated.** ICO and EDPS recruitment guidance applies
data minimisation to the **employer** — what they may collect and ask for. It
places no obligation on a candidate volunteering their own data. GDPR is
therefore a reason employers stopped *asking* for photos and dates of birth; it
is not a rule against you including them. Anyone telling Pradeep "GDPR means you
can't put a photo on your CV" is wrong about the mechanism.

**What this means for Pradeep specifically.** His actual target set — DeepMind
London, Apple Munich/Zurich, NVIDIA Europe, Amazon Development Centers, ASML,
Google/Meta European offices, AI scaleups in Amsterdam/Berlin/Zurich — are
international-tech employers running global ATS pipelines with recruiting
standards set in the US. They are precisely the segment where the traditional
personal-data block is absent and where a photo is at best neutral and at worst
an ATS parsing hazard (ETH's own ATS tips say *"do not use extravagant
formatting, graphics, images or logos"* — a photo is a graphic).

Concrete position:

- **No photo.** Neutral-to-negative at every one of his targets, an ATS risk, and
  the German federal anti-discrimination agency is on record against it. The only
  segment where it helps is traditional Swiss/German local firms, which are not
  his primary market.
- **No date of birth, no marital status, no children.** Zero upside at
  international employers; real downside (age filtering) everywhere; formally
  "optional" even in ETH's own checklist.
- **Keep nationality — but only because it is load-bearing for work
  authorisation** (see §2), not as a personal-data convention.
- Do **not** maintain a photo-bearing "Swiss/German variant." The maintenance
  cost and the risk of the wrong file going out exceeds the marginal benefit at a
  segment he is not primarily targeting. If a specific traditional employer
  requests one, add it for that application.

### 1.3 Europass: don't, unless the employer is an EU institution

Sourcing here is genuinely thin and I am flagging it rather than padding it. Every
"Europass is bad" article surfaced was a resume mill or template seller and none
met the source bar.

What can be established:

- **Europass is absent from ETH's 83-page 2025 Application Guide — zero
  mentions.** By contrast the same document mentions LinkedIn 24 times and
  Applicant Tracking Systems 23 times. Europe's leading technical university's
  official careers guidance does not consider Europass part of the application
  toolkit at all. This is negative evidence, but from a strong source.
- Europass's own positioning (europass.europa.eu, EURES) frames it around
  mobility, education and skills transparency, not competitive private-sector
  hiring. EU institutions and agencies *do* mandate it for their own vacancies —
  e.g. the European Environment Agency publishes a Europass template as a
  required application document.

**What this means for Pradeep specifically:** use Europass only if the employer
is an EU institution/agency or explicitly demands the template. It is not a
scarlet letter, but its rigid grid is structurally hostile to the one thing that
makes his CV work — dense, specific, quantified engineering claims with named
constraints. A Europass CV would flatten "8 cm → 3 cm MPJPE, +30% under
occlusion, verified bit-identical by weight diff" into a skills-level checkbox.

### 1.4 The "Europeans hate American superlatives" claim is mostly a myth — but there is a real version of it

ETH, directly:

> "There is no fundamental difference between the content of American resumes and
> cover letters and European applications. However, you should be aware that the
> priorities are different from those in our latitudes."

And its own CV instructions are achievement-oriented in exactly the way US
guidance is: *"Focus on actions and outcomes (projects, activities, results)"*;
*"Describe your activities precisely and expressively"*; *"Use keywords found in
the job ad and in the company's vocabulary."* It also warns: *"Write in your own
style; do not recycle pre-worded sentences found online or in guides."*

So the myth is that quantified achievement is culturally unwelcome. It isn't.
What differs is the **formal apparatus**, not the register:

- Complete month/year records for every role, degree and course. ETH's checklist
  repeats *"a complete record of months and years"* for work experience,
  education and continuing education. Gaps are noticed in a way they are not in
  US screening.
- A **language section with consistent levels for every language including the
  mother tongue**. ETH: *"state your native language"*; *"indicate the skill level
  clearly and consistently for all languages"*; order by relevance to the job.
- **References**: *"Do not enclose or mention references by name unless
  specifically requested to do so."* Note availability on request.
- **Cover letter, max one page**, still genuinely read across continental Europe
  — unlike US tech, where it is often ignored. ETH gives it a full chapter and a
  YOU–ME–WE structure.
- Certificates/references (*Arbeitszeugnisse*) are part of a complete dossier in
  German-speaking markets.
- Send **CV and cover letter as separate PDF files**.

**What this means for Pradeep specifically:** his existing style — verifiable
numbers, named failure modes, no unfalsifiable adjectives — is *more* aligned with
European register than typical US resume-speak, not less. He should change
nothing about tone. What he must add for Europe:

1. Month/year precision on all four positions and the MSc. No bare years.
2. A real **Languages** section. This is not filler for him: Tamil/English plus
   whatever his actual Mandarin level is after five years in Taiwan is a genuine
   differentiator for a European employer, and the ability to state it at a
   defensible CEFR level is worth more than an extra skills line. (His exact
   Mandarin level is unknown to this research — see §6.)
3. A one-page cover letter that carries the relocation narrative and the
   attribution nuance the CV can only state flatly.

### 1.5 ATS is not a US-only concern

ETH devotes a section to Applicant Tracking Systems and its advice is identical
to the US-derived findings already in `ats-mechanics.md`:

> "Do not put contact information in a header because the software may be set so
> that a document's headers and footers are not displayed."
> "If possible, use standard fonts like Arial, Calibri, Helvetica, Tahoma or
> Verdana."
> "The font size should be at least 11 pt, not smaller."
> "Do not use extravagant formatting, graphics, images or logos."
> "Do not format information in tables as these may go unrecognized or in
> graphical elements (e.g. empty and filled circles or boxes for level
> information)."

**What this means for Pradeep specifically:** the existing PDF pipeline
(`src/lib/resumePdf.tsx` — Helvetica, single column, contact in body, no tables,
no icons carrying meaning) is already correct for Europe with no changes. Two
things to verify: body font is ≥11 pt (ETH's floor is stricter than typical US
guidance, which tolerates 10 pt), and skill levels are never rendered as
dots/bars.

## 2. Work authorisation — the structural facts and a defensible CV rule

### 2.1 The instruments that actually apply to him

**EU Blue Card** (Directive 2021/1883, in force in 25 member states — not Denmark
or Ireland). Requires a work contract or binding offer for highly qualified
employment of **at least six months**; a tertiary qualification of ≥3 years
(ISCED 6+); salary between **1 and 1.6× the national average gross**. The revised
directive added that **professional experience may substitute for a degree in ICT
specifically**, and grants **intra-EU mobility after 12 months** without a fresh
labour-market test.

Pradeep qualifies on education via the NTUT MSc, so the ICT-experience clause is
a fallback rather than his route. The 12-month mobility right matters
strategically: **the first EU role is the hard one; the second is procedurally
much easier.** That is an argument for optimising the first move for
*obtainability* rather than for perfect fit.

**Germany (2026 thresholds, Bundesagentur für Arbeit / Make it in Germany):**
standard Blue Card **€50,700** gross p.a.; shortage-occupation and new-entrant
threshold **€45,934.20** (45.3% of the contribution assessment ceiling), which
requires Federal Employment Agency approval. AI/CV salaries in Munich/Berlin sit
above both, so salary is not his binding constraint in Germany.

**Netherlands (IND, H2 2026):** highly skilled migrant aged 30+ **€5,942/month**
excluding holiday allowance; under 30 **€4,357**; EU Blue Card **€5,942**, reduced
Blue Card **€4,754**. Critically, the Dutch route runs through **IND *recognised
sponsors***, and the IND publishes a **public register of them, updated
monthly**. An employer not on that register cannot hire him through the fast
route.

> **Directly actionable:** filter every Dutch target against the IND public
> register *before* applying. ASML, Philips, Booking, Adyen, TomTom and the large
> scaleups are recognised sponsors; many smaller Amsterdam startups are not.
> Applying to a non-sponsor is a guaranteed dead end regardless of CV quality.

**Switzerland — the biggest structural finding.** Third-country nationals are
subject to **hard national quotas**: for 2026 the Federal Council set **4,500 B
(residence) permits and 4,000 L (short-stay) permits** for the whole country,
unchanged from 2025 (~52% used by late September 2025). On top of the quota, the
employer must satisfy a **priority-of-nationals labour market test** — proving no
suitable candidate exists in Switzerland or the EU/EFTA — and only *highly
qualified* candidates (degree plus substantial experience) are admitted at all.

**What this means for Pradeep specifically:** Zurich (Google, Apple, Meta, NVIDIA,
DeepMind Zurich, ETH-adjacent) is materially harder for an Indian national than
Amsterdam, Munich, Berlin or Dublin. He should not drop Switzerland, but he must
understand what the CV is doing there: the employer has to *build a case* to a
cantonal authority that no EU/EFTA candidate could do this job. His
externally-verifiable differentiators — NVIDIA GTC poster, the 7,000-store
deployment, the TAITRA global win — are not vanity items in that context; **they
are the raw material of the employer's own permit justification.** They must be
in the top third of page one, not buried in a late "Awards" section.

**UK — Global Talent visa (digital technology).** No job offer required;
self-sponsored; endorsed by Tech Nation on behalf of the Home Office. Applicants
must meet a **mandatory criterion** (recognised by others as a leading talent —
"Exceptional Talent" — or a potential leading talent — "Exceptional Promise" — in
digital technology) **plus at least 2 optional criteria**, which include:
innovation as an employee working in a new digital technology field or concept;
significant technical, commercial or entrepreneurial contributions in a
product-led digital technology company; contribution to the sector outside work;
and **research published or endorsed by an expert**. Exceptional Promise applicants
are *"likely to have less than 5 years of experience in technology."* Cost £766 +
IHS (~£1,035/yr). Endorsement decision typically 5–8 weeks.

**What this means for Pradeep specifically — this may be the highest-leverage
finding in the entire document.** On the face of it he plausibly satisfies the
mandatory criterion and at least three optional ones: employee-innovation in a new
field (the frozen-pathway architecture, on-device 3D lifting); significant
technical contributions in a product-led company (XView AI shipped to PGA
professionals; the 7-ELEVEN system); and research published/endorsed (the
*Scientific Reports* paper describing his system, the NVIDIA GTC poster). His ~3
years in AI puts him naturally on the **Exceptional Promise** track, though his
eight years total engineering could be argued either way.

If endorsed, he becomes a **sponsor-free candidate in the UK**. That removes the
single largest filter applied to his applications and lets the CV carry the line
*"Right to work in the UK (Global Talent) — no sponsorship required."* No
wording change to any bullet is worth as much as that line. **This is a
several-week parallel workstream that should start independently of the CV.**

### 2.2 Should the CV mention work authorisation at all?

The trusted sources converge on "yes," from different directions:

- **ETH (Swiss employer-facing, mandatory framing):** *"nationality and residence
  permit (for foreign nationals)"*; *"If you have a work permit, mention it."*
- **Imperial College Business School careers:** *"State your citizenship or
  current visa permissions clearly to avoid any uncertainty for the hiring
  team,"* noting European firms prefer to know upfront.
- **ICO / EDPS:** data minimisation constrains the *employer's* collection, not
  the candidate's disclosure. No legal obstacle to volunteering it.
- Against, but not to the point of "hide it": the German AGG framework and the
  Antidiskriminierungsstelle's position that nationality disclosed at screening
  stage is exactly where discrimination occurs.

**No trusted source recommends concealment.** The recommendation below is
therefore about *framing*, which is where the sources are silent and where the
reasoning has to do the work.

> **Rule: state work authorisation as an operational fact with a named
> mechanism — never as a request.**

Recruiters do not reject "requires sponsorship" primarily on cost. They reject it
on **uncertainty** — an unknown process, an unknown timeline, an unknown
probability of failure. Naming the specific legal instrument converts an unknown
into a known, bounded, routine procedure that their mobility team has run before.

Phrasing, by market:

- **Generic EU / Germany / most targets** — one line in the contact block:
  `Indian national · MSc (EU-recognised) · eligible for EU Blue Card · currently
  on a Taiwan work permit`

> **CORRECTION (2026-08-15), added after this file was written.** The clause
> "currently on a Taiwan work permit" above is factually wrong. Pradeep holds a
> Taiwan **APRC** (Alien Permanent Resident Certificate) — permanent residency,
> with open work rights and no employer tie. This file was written before his
> residency status was known. The distinction matters: an APRC makes a remote
> arrangement from Taiwan structurally simple rather than sponsorship-dependent.
> Whether any of it belongs on the CV is still an open research question.
- **Netherlands** — same, but only send to IND recognised sponsors; the cover
  letter can name the highly skilled migrant route explicitly.
- **UK, once endorsed** — `Right to work in the UK (Global Talent visa) — no
  sponsorship required.` Until then, omit UK-specific claims entirely.
- **Switzerland** — do not lead with it. The quota problem is the employer's to
  solve; the CV's job is to make him worth a quota slot. Nationality goes in the
  personal-details block per local convention and the cover letter carries the
  rest.
- **Never** write "seeking visa sponsorship" or "open to sponsorship." Both read
  as an ask and invite a no.

**The relocation-risk argument belongs in the cover letter, and he is unusually
well placed to make it.** He has already executed one international relocation
(India → Taiwan), completed a degree in a second language environment, and held a
foreign work permit for five years across two employers. Most sponsorship
hesitancy is fear of a failed relocation, not paperwork cost. He has a five-year
track record disproving exactly that fear, and it is currently doing no work on
his behalf.

## 3. How research-engineering hybrid profiles are evaluated

### 3.1 The evidence

- **Google DeepMind Research Engineer postings (London).** Minimum qualifications
  are degree + years of technical/engineering experience (e.g. Bachelor's or
  equivalent practical experience for some; Master's/PhD for others; 5 years
  technical experience for Embodied Agents). **Publications appear under
  *preferred*, not *minimum*, qualifications.** The role is framed as the bridge
  between theory and implementation — designing, building and scaling systems to
  test research ideas.
- **Siemens senior R&D (medical imaging AI):** requires *"a track record of
  publications **or presentations** at top-tier conferences, journals, **or
  competitions**."* Note both disjunctions. A presented poster is a presentation.
  A global competition win is a competition result.
- **ASML Fellowship Program:** technical experts recognised *"as proven by a
  track record of publications **and** patents."* At the top of the European
  industrial ladder, patents count alongside papers.
- **Qualcomm computer-vision roles:** *"publication, patent, or external
  technical contribution experience is a plus"* — explicitly a plus, explicitly
  disjunctive, explicitly including non-publication contributions.
- **Apple research-engineer CV roles** reportedly frame it as *"publications in
  top-tier journals or conferences, patents, or impactful industry experience."*
  **Unverified verbatim** — the posting had expired by the time of fetching. Treat
  as second-hand.
- **ETH industry-vs-academia CV table** (industry column): state the thesis title,
  details only if relevant; methods and lab experience only if relevant;
  ***"Do not list conferences in your CV. If relevant, attach a complete list as a
  separate document"***; do not name professors.

### 3.2 What this means for Pradeep specifically

**The "no first-author top-tier paper" anxiety is misplaced for the track his
profile actually fits.** Publications gate the Research *Scientist* title. They do
not gate Research *Engineer*, which is the archetypal role for exactly his shape:
strong systems engineering plus genuine ML depth, MSc rather than PhD, evidence in
shipped systems rather than in citations. He should apply to Research Engineer /
Senior ML Engineer / Applied Scientist(Engineering-track) titles and stop
benchmarking himself against a Research Scientist bar he is not applying to.

Substitute evidence, in the order European industrial employers appear to weight
it:

1. **Shipped systems at scale, with the constraint named.** 7,000+ stores;
   on-device CoreML on Apple ARM; a product in professional use. The constraint is
   what makes it research-adjacent rather than plumbing — "on-device" and
   "provably unbiased" are the load-bearing words, not "deployed."
2. **Third-party validation that is not self-reported.** NVIDIA GTC 2025 poster;
   the *Scientific Reports* paper about the system; TAITRA (1 of 3 from 638
   proposals, 55 countries); Taiwan Expo Europe 2026 selection. In a European
   context this class of evidence does double duty — it also feeds the visa
   justification (§2.1, Switzerland).
3. **Named, non-obvious engineering decisions.** The frozen-pathway architecture
   composing Progressive Neural Networks / Side-Tuning / ControlNet zero-conv
   ideas, verified bit-identical by weight diff; the deterministic-grounding LLM
   design (69 rules, zero LLM-computed numbers). **This is the part most likely to
   be under-sold and it is the part that reads as *research* to a research
   engineer interviewer.** Metrics alone read as engineering; a named architectural
   choice with a stated reason reads as research.
4. **Metrics paired with the failure mode they were designed to catch.** His
   through-line — "a metric that cannot see a fault will certify it" — is the
   single most differentiating sentence in his material. It belongs in the profile
   line or the cover letter, stated once, not diffused across bullets.

**The GTC poster and ETH's "do not list conferences" rule do not conflict** —
but the CV must make that obvious. ETH is warning against listing conference
*attendance*, which is not an output. A presented poster is an output. He should
place it under a heading like **"Selected external output & recognition,"** never
under "Conferences," so it cannot be misread as attendance padding.

**The concrete gap.** He has no patent, no open-source artefact, and no public
technical writeup a stranger can inspect. Everything in category 3 above is
currently "trust me." Ranked by return-per-effort for the European market:

1. **A filed patent application naming him as inventor.** ASML and Qualcomm treat
   patents as first-class equivalents to publications; a filing is a verifiable
   third-party artefact and is far more attainable for him than a first-author
   CVPR paper. **He should check whether IdeasLab Formosa or President Information
   Corp have filed anything naming him** — this may already exist and be missing
   from his CV.
2. **One substantive public technical writeup or repo** on the frozen-pathway
   architecture or the deterministic-grounding design. Converts category 3 from
   assertion to inspectable.
3. A co-authored workshop paper — real, but slow and lowest-yield of the three.

## 4. The honest-attribution problem

This is the section he cares most about, and it is the one where the sourcing is
strongest — because the right sources are not career blogs but **research
integrity standards**, which are precisely the norms a European industrial
research hiring manager operates inside.

### 4.1 The standards

**ALLEA, *The European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity*, revised edition
2023** — designated by the European Commission as the primary standard for
research integrity across all EU-funded research. Section 3.1, among unacceptable
practices:

> "**Misrepresenting research achievements, data, involvement, or interests.**"

The word **"involvement"** is doing exactly the work he needs. Claiming a paper
you did not author is misrepresenting involvement, and the Code names it. Equally
important, the Code's preamble states that research is *"carried out by many
different actors in academic, industry, and other settings"* — so this is **not
an academia-only norm.** It binds industrial research claims, which is why it is
the right frame for his case rather than generic CV advice.

Section 2.7 establishes that non-authorship contribution is a **recognised,
legitimate category**, not a grey zone:

> "Authors acknowledge important work and contributions of those who do not meet
> the criteria for authorship, including collaborators, assistants, and funders
> who have enabled the research."

**CRediT — Contributor Roles Taxonomy, ANSI/NISO Z39.104-2022.** A formal
standard of 14 contributor roles, adopted by 50+ publishers, designed explicitly
to *"replace the binary author / non-author model with structured per-role
attribution."* The roles that describe what he actually did: **Software,
Methodology, Investigation, Validation, Conceptualization, Resources.**

**University of Cambridge authorship guidance / eLife** reinforce that
below-authorship contributors are acknowledged, and that acknowledgements should
*"fully reflect the level of the input of the contributor."*

### 4.2 Rules for his CV

**Rule 1 — the paper must never appear under a "Publications" heading.** A
"Publications" heading is a claim of authorship regardless of how the entry is
worded; no amount of careful phrasing inside it undoes the heading. Use
"Selected external output & recognition," or fold it into the role bullet
entirely. This one structural choice does more work than any wording change.

**Rule 2 — attribute at the *system* level; cite the paper as documentation of
the system's results, not as a credential of his.** Pattern: *[what he built] →
[what it does] → [the third-party document reporting it, with his relation
stated]*.

> Lead engineer, shelf-detection and few-shot product-recognition system deployed
> across 7,000+ 7-ELEVEN stores in Taiwan. System and results subsequently
> published by the company in *Scientific Reports* (Dec 2025) — 99.23% precision
> / 98.93% recall shelf detection; 98.39% Top-1 on unseen products. I was lead
> engineer on the system, not an author on the paper.

The explicit non-authorship clause is **not** something any source recommends —
flagging that honestly. But it does precisely what he wants: it eliminates
ambiguity, it is unfalsifiable in his favour (nobody has ever lied in that
direction), and to a research-literate European reader it signals calibration
rather than deficit. A softer variant carrying the same information: *"System
described in the company's peer-reviewed paper (Sci Rep, 2025); my role was lead
engineer on the system."*

**Rule 3 — use CRediT vocabulary when a role needs describing.** *"Software and
methodology contributor to the system reported in…"* is standards-backed,
precise, and instantly legible to anyone from a research environment. It is also
**checkable**, which is the point.

> **Highest-value open action in this document: he must check whether he is named
> in the *Scientific Reports* paper's acknowledgements section.** If he is,
> everything changes — "acknowledged in [paper]" is a verifiable third-party
> statement about his involvement, published in a peer-reviewed venue, and it
> should be quoted directly rather than asserted. ALLEA §2.7 is the norm that
> makes such an acknowledgement meaningful rather than a consolation prize.

**Rule 4 — TAITRA: separate the *authorship of the proposal* from the *receipt of
the prize*.** Two distinct facts, both true, and only one is his.

> Sole author of the technical proposal that won TAITRA "Go Healthy with Taiwan"
> 2025 — 1 of 3 winners from 638 proposals across 55 countries (award to IdeasLab
> Formosa; USD 30,000).

Company-in-parentheses is the honest construction: it credits the organisation
with the award while claiming only the writing. Never use "won" with himself as
subject; *"authored the winning proposal"* is both more accurate **and**
stronger, because writing a proposal that beats 637 others is a specific,
attributable skill whereas "won an award" is ambient.

**Rule 5 — the general principle: claim the verb, not the noun.** He owns
verbs — *designed, built, led, authored, validated, reduced, verified*. He does
not own nouns belonging to the organisation — *the paper, the award, the
7,000-store footprint, the PGA relationship*. Every bullet should have him as the
subject of a verb he personally performed, with org-owned nouns appearing as
objects or context. This is the mechanically reliable version of "don't blur the
line," and it generalises to cases this document didn't anticipate.

**Rule 6 — the K.J. Choi / PGA detail is company context, not achievement.** It is
genuinely impressive and genuinely not his. It belongs in the company descriptor,
one clause, once — *"IdeasLab Formosa (golf technology; advisors include K.J.
Choi, 9× PGA Tour winner)"* — and never inside an achievement bullet. Same error
class as the award. The *product* fact — "iOS product in use by PGA
professionals" — **is** his, because he shipped it.

**Rule 7 — under-claiming is also misrepresentation.** ALLEA's wording is
"misrepresenting … **involvement**," and that cuts both ways. If he was the lead
engineer, writing "contributed to" is *inaccurate* and costs him the role. The
standard is **precision, not modesty.** His instinct to refuse blurring is
correct; the failure mode to watch is over-correction into vagueness, which reads

## 6. Open questions and gaps — stated rather than padded

**Sourcing gaps in this research:**

1. **Europass as an active negative signal in tech: unsourced.** Every critical
   article found was a resume mill. The recommendation in §1.3 rests on negative
   evidence (ETH's 83-page guide never mentions it) plus Europass's own
   positioning. That is enough to justify "don't use it," but not enough to
   justify "it will hurt you."
2. **Nordics beyond Denmark: thin.** Workindenmark is authoritative for Denmark.
   Equivalent Swedish and Norwegian guidance on photos/DOB was not located at the
   required source quality. Copenhagen guidance should not be generalised to
   Stockholm or Oslo without checking.
3. **NVP Sollicitatiecode: low confidence.** The official PDF would not extract
   text; findings rest on a secondary summary. The Dutch-specific personal-data
   rules should be re-verified before relying on them.
4. **Apple's "publications, patents, or impactful industry experience" phrasing:
   unverified verbatim** — the posting had expired. Qualcomm's and Siemens'
   equivalent disjunctive phrasing *was* verified and carries the same point.
5. **No source found that addresses how European employers weight a
   *non-authored* paper.** The entire framework in §4 is assembled from research
   integrity standards (ALLEA, CRediT, Cambridge) applied to a CV context. That
   is a reasoned extrapolation, not a documented hiring practice. It is
   defensible — those are the norms the readers operate under — but it is
   extrapolation and should be labelled as such if challenged.
6. **DeepMind JD wording is from search summaries, not verbatim fetch** (the
   Google Careers pages did not render). The claim "publications are preferred,
   not minimum" is consistent across several postings but was not quoted directly.

**Facts about Pradeep this research needs and does not have:**

7. **Is he named in the *Scientific Reports* acknowledgements?** Highest-value
   unknown in the document. Changes the attribution strategy materially (§4.3).
8. **Do IdeasLab Formosa or President Information Corp have patent filings naming
   him as an inventor?** If yes, this is missing from his CV and is first-class
   evidence at ASML/Qualcomm-tier European employers (§3.2).
9. **How is he listed on the NVIDIA GTC 2025 poster** — presenter, author,
   co-author? The CV must mirror the official listing exactly.
10. **His age.** Determines which IND salary band applies (€4,357 under 30 vs
    €5,942 at 30+) and therefore which Dutch roles are even viable.
11. **His actual Mandarin level (CEFR or TOCFL).** The Languages section is a
    real differentiator for him in Europe and needs a defensible level, not
    "conversational."
12. **Whether he has already begun, or would consider, a UK Global Talent
    application.** §2.1 argues this is the highest-leverage single action
    available; it is a multi-week process that should run in parallel with, not
    after, the CV work.
